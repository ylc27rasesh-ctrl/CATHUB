Module 00 · Orientation

The other
thirty per cent.

Verbal Ability — para-jumbles, para-summary, odd-sentence — accounts for roughly seven or eight VARC marks. Faster than RC, riskier than RC, and pure-deduction in a way RC never is.

Verbal Ability is the smaller, sharper half of VARC. Where RC asks for patience and interpretation, VA asks for something closer to logic: every question has a single correct answer that follows from a small set of grammatical and rhetorical principles, and a student who knows the principles can attempt each question in under two minutes. The skill is structural pattern-recognition — recognising openers, finding transition cues, distinguishing summaries from extensions — and once recognised, the answers fall out cleanly.

The current CAT VA section has settled around three or four standard formats: para-jumble (a paragraph's sentences are presented out of order, you arrange them), para-summary (a paragraph is presented in full, you select the best summary among four options), odd-sentence-out (five sentences are given, four belong to a single paragraph, one does not — identify the outlier), and increasingly the older sentence-completion and critical-reasoning formats appearing in mock papers. This codex covers all of these, beginning with para-jumbles, which is both the most common and the most teachable.

0.1What VA tests

One core capacity, tested four different ways: the ability to recognise paragraph-level coherence. A coherent paragraph has an opening sentence that sets up the topic, middle sentences that develop it, and a closing sentence that resolves or refines it. The transitions between sentences are signalled — by pronouns, by connective adverbs, by repeated nouns. The VA examiner gives you a paragraph with the signals intact and asks you to use them.

The VA maxim. Coherence is in the connectives, not the content. If you read the sentences without tracking the connecting cues — pronouns, demonstratives, contrast words — you are guessing, not solving.

0.2How this codex is organised

Module 01 covers para-jumbles in depth — opener identification, mandatory pairs, transition words, the order-of-deduction protocol. Module 02 handles para-summary, with emphasis on scope and tone matching. Module 03 covers odd-sentence-out, which is essentially para-jumble in reverse. Module 04 is the older sentence-completion format, with sub-types like single-blank and double-blank. Module 05 takes a closer look at connectors — the small set of words that signal almost every transition CAT cares about. Module 06 covers the critical-reasoning format that occasionally appears.

Module 07 lists the ten classic VA traps. Module 08 is the problem bank: 25 questions distributed across the four formats with the full reasoning shown. Module 09 collapses the recognition signals onto one reference page.

Pacing. VA questions are TITA (type-in-the-answer) in CAT — no options for jumble and odd-sentence. A wrong answer carries no negative marking, but the time cost is what hurts. Spend two minutes max per question; if stuck, mark and move on.

Module 01 · Para-jumbles

The order-restoration
puzzle.

Four or five sentences from a coherent paragraph, scrambled. Restore the original order. The strategy: find the opener, find the mandatory pairs, fit everything around them.

Para-jumbles are the canonical VA question. The CAT has been setting them, in one form or another, since the late 1990s, and despite occasional format tweaks the underlying skill has not changed. A coherent paragraph is being broken apart; you re-assemble it using the textual cues — pronouns, connectives, repeated terms — that signal which sentence follows which.

1.1The four-step protocol

  1. Identify the opener. The opening sentence introduces the topic without referring back to anything. Look for: a noun that everyone else refers to with pronouns, a general claim that the others elaborate, an absence of "this", "that", "however", "moreover" at the start.
  2. Find mandatory pairs. A mandatory pair is two sentences where one must follow the other directly — usually because the second contains a pronoun whose referent is in the first, or a connective like "however" that responds to the first. Mandatory pairs are unbreakable; the order between them is fixed.
  3. Find the closer. The closing sentence often contains a conclusion-marker ("thus", "in this way", "ultimately") or a forward-looking statement. It rarely opens a new sub-topic.
  4. Slot the rest. The middle sentences fit between opener and closer in the order that preserves the mandatory pairs and respects the topic flow.

1.2Opener identification

The most efficient way to find the opener is by elimination. A sentence that cannot be the opener is one that begins with a pronoun without a clear preceding referent, a demonstrative ("this", "such", "these"), or a transition word ("however", "moreover", "consequently"). Eliminate these from the candidate set; the remaining sentence is often the opener.

Opener disqualifiers Starts with "This/That/Such/These" — refers back.
Starts with "However/But/Yet/Nevertheless" — contradicts something.
Starts with "Moreover/Furthermore/Also/Additionally" — extends something.
Starts with "Consequently/Thus/Hence/Therefore" — concludes something.
Starts with "He/She/It/They" without a noun antecedent — refers to a previous sentence.

1.3Mandatory pairs

Two sentences are paired when the second cannot stand without the first. The most reliable indicator is a pronoun in sentence Y whose only natural referent is in sentence X — Y must follow X. Other indicators:

  • Y begins with "however" and X states the position Y contradicts.
  • Y begins with "for example" and X states the claim Y illustrates.
  • X ends with a noun and Y begins with "this [same noun]" or "such [noun]".
  • Y begins with "in contrast" or "by comparison" — X provides the thing being contrasted.

1.4Worked example

1
However, the more revolutionary claim — that fragmentation actually expanded the public sphere by enabling micro-communities to form — has gained ground in the past decade.
2
For most of the past twenty years, scholars of digital media argued that the internet had fragmented public discourse rather than uniting it.
3
This newer view treats the fragmentation not as a loss but as a structural feature of any genuinely plural society.
4
The consensus, by 2010, was that this fragmentation was the dominant intellectual effect of the platforms.

Step 1: Opener. Sentence 2 is the only one without back-referring cues. It introduces the topic ("scholars argued that"). 1 starts with "However" — disqualified. 3 starts with "This newer view" — refers to something prior. 4 contains "this fragmentation" — refers to fragmentation just introduced. Opener = 2.

Step 2: Mandatory pairs. 4 mentions "this fragmentation" — must follow 2 (which introduces fragmentation). So 2 → 4 is a pair. 1 says "However, the more revolutionary claim…" — this contrasts with the "consensus" mentioned in 4. So 4 → 1. 3 says "This newer view" — referring to the "more revolutionary claim" in 1. So 1 → 3.

Step 3: Order. 2 → 4 → 1 → 3.

2 → 4
"this fragmentation" in 4 refers to fragmentation introduced in 2
4 → 1
"However" in 1 contrasts with the "consensus" view stated in 4
1 → 3
"This newer view" in 3 refers to the "revolutionary claim" in 1

Final order: 2, 4, 1, 3.

The pattern. Almost every para-jumble has at least one pair of sentences whose order is fixed by a pronoun or transition word. Find that pair first. The rest of the puzzle is usually decidable from there.

Module 02 · Para-summary

The compress-without-distorting
question.

A paragraph is given in full. Four candidate summaries follow. Pick the one that captures the central claim without adding, omitting, or distorting.

Para-summary tests something subtle: the ability to compress a paragraph by a factor of three or four without losing the central claim or introducing anything not in the original. The wrong answers are usually wrong in characteristic ways — too narrow (covers only half the paragraph), too broad (loses the specific stance), distorted (shifts the emphasis), or out-of-scope (adds details not in the original).

2.1The two-question test

Para-summary criteria Coverage: Does the summary capture the central claim of the paragraph as a whole?
Fidelity: Does it avoid adding anything not in the paragraph, and avoid changing the emphasis or stance?

The correct summary is the one that passes both tests. The three wrong summaries fail at least one — usually fidelity (they add or omit) more often than coverage.

2.2The wrong-answer flavours

Too narrow

Captures only the first or last sentence of the paragraph. Misses the connecting thread. A summary that mentions only one of two opposing views in a two-view paragraph is too narrow.

Too broad

Could summarise any paragraph on the same topic. Misses the specific argument or stance of this paragraph. "The paragraph discusses X" is almost always too broad.

Distorted

Captures the topic but shifts the stance — the paragraph favours X, the summary suggests neutrality, or vice versa. Watch the verbs in particular: "challenges" vs "presents", "rejects" vs "considers".

Out-of-scope

Adds a prediction, recommendation, or inference that the paragraph itself never makes. If the paragraph stops at "the consequences are unclear", the summary cannot end with "the policy should therefore be reconsidered".

2.3The scope and tone match

Beyond coverage and fidelity, the summary must match the paragraph in scope (how broadly the claim is asserted) and tone (whether the paragraph is critical, approving, neutral). A paragraph that mildly criticises a policy should not be summarised by a sentence that strongly rejects it.

2.4Worked example

Paragraph: "The doctrine that economic growth is the principal route out of poverty has been the foundation of development thinking for over half a century. There is much to recommend it: countries that have grown rapidly have generally seen the most dramatic reductions in absolute poverty. Yet the dependence on growth has obscured an inconvenient fact — that some forms of growth, particularly those concentrated in capital-intensive sectors, can leave large parts of the population materially worse off in relative terms even as overall national income rises. The complication is not that growth fails to reduce poverty; it is that the type of growth matters as much as the rate."

Candidate summaries:

  • (A) Economic growth has been the central concept in development thinking and should remain so.
  • (B) Capital-intensive sectors are bad for poverty reduction.
  • (C) While economic growth has been broadly successful in reducing poverty, the type of growth matters as much as the rate.
  • (D) Development thinking has been dominated by economists for too long and needs revision.

Test each:

  • (A) Misses the central qualification — that type matters. Distorted.
  • (B) Too narrow — only the capital-intensive parenthetical, missing the main claim.
  • (C) Captures both halves: growth has worked, but type matters. Best match.
  • (D) Out-of-scope — paragraph doesn't critique economists or call for revision; it refines the growth doctrine.

Answer: (C).

The compression test. A good summary preserves the paragraph's argumentative structure: claim-plus-qualification stays claim-plus-qualification; two-views stays two-views. If a paragraph has a "yet" or "however", the summary almost always needs the same word.

Module 03 · Odd-Sentence-Out

The intruder
question.

Five sentences are given. Four belong to a coherent paragraph; one does not. Identify the outlier. The strategy: build the four-sentence paragraph and see which sentence refuses to fit.

Odd-sentence-out is structurally para-jumble's mirror image. Where the jumble asks you to find the right ordering of the right sentences, the odd-sentence asks you to identify which sentence doesn't belong at all. The skill is the same — detection of paragraph coherence — but the task is filtration rather than ordering.

3.1How outliers look

The intruder sentence is usually on the same broad topic as the paragraph — same word, same setting — but makes a different kind of claim. Common varieties:

  • The off-topic specific. The paragraph is about urban migration in general; the intruder describes a particular Mumbai neighbourhood. Specific where the paragraph is general.
  • The wrong-frame intruder. The paragraph treats the topic from a sociological angle; the intruder approaches it from a personal-anecdote angle.
  • The contradictory. The paragraph defends a thesis; the intruder makes a claim that contradicts the thesis without being framed as a counter-argument.
  • The chronologically wrong. The paragraph discusses events in one historical period; the intruder belongs to a different period.

3.2The filtration protocol

  1. Read all five sentences quickly to grasp the broad topic.
  2. Identify which sentence is the opener — the most general, no back-references.
  3. Try to thread the remaining four into a paragraph that flows. The one that consistently refuses to fit is the odd sentence.
  4. Verify by writing out the four-sentence paragraph: if it reads as coherent, the fifth is the outlier.

3.3Worked example

1
The rise of digital media in the 2000s transformed how political campaigns reached voters in democratic societies.
2
Television, which had dominated political communication since the 1960s, lost much of its monopoly to social networks and online platforms.
3
My local political party's social media manager once told me that voter engagement online tripled after they hired a video editor.
4
By 2016, more than two-thirds of voters in the United States and Western Europe reported encountering political content primarily through social media rather than broadcast.
5
This shift produced a measurable change in how campaigns budgeted: spending on broadcast television declined while spending on targeted digital advertising rose sharply.

Identify topic. Sentences 1, 2, 4, 5 all describe the macro-shift from broadcast to digital political communication. Sentence 3 is a personal anecdote from "my local political party's social media manager".

Test fit. Sentences 1, 2, 4, 5 thread together cleanly: 1 (overview) → 2 (TV-vs-digital framing) → 4 (numerical evidence by 2016) → 5 (budget consequences). Sentence 3 sits in a different register entirely — anecdotal, personal, single-case.

Outlier: 3. The wrong-frame intruder.

The register cue. Many odd-sentences are betrayed by a shift in register — a paragraph that has been analytical suddenly contains "I", "my", or a specific named individual. Register shifts are a strong tell, even if the topic is the same.

Module 04 · Sentence Completion

The fit-the-blank
question.

A sentence has one or two blanks. Four pairs of words are offered as candidates. Pick the pair that completes the sentence with the right meaning and the right register.

Sentence completion was once a CAT staple and has reappeared in recent mock papers and shadow papers. The format is straightforward but unforgiving: a sentence with one or two blanks, four option-pairs to fill them. The correct option is the one that makes the sentence grammatically sound, semantically coherent, and tonally consistent.

4.1The three tests

Sentence-completion criteria Grammar: Does the word fit the syntactic slot? (Noun vs verb, singular vs plural, tense.)
Sense: Does the resulting sentence mean something coherent in context?
Register: Does the word's tone match the surrounding language? Don't insert "vexed" into a casual sentence or "annoyed" into a literary one.

4.2The double-blank protocol

For two-blank sentences, both blanks must work for the option to be correct. The strategy is to identify the easier blank first (often the second, since it has more context), eliminate options that fail there, then test the remaining options on the harder blank.

4.3Vocabulary scope

CAT sentence-completion vocabulary tends towards formal but not arcane: words a moderately literate reader of newspapers and serious magazines would know. The examiner avoids both colloquialism and obscure technical terms. Build vocabulary by reading editorial pages of broadsheets, literary supplements, and policy journals.

4.4Worked example

Sentence: The senator's _____ response to the journalist's question — neither denying the accusation nor admitting it — only _____ the impression that something was being concealed.

  • (A) evasive — reinforced
  • (B) blunt — diminished
  • (C) thoughtful — extended
  • (D) heated — confirmed

Test the first blank: "neither denying nor admitting" describes evasion. (A) evasive ✓. (B) blunt — would deny or admit, not both — fails. (C) thoughtful — possible but doesn't match "neither/nor" pattern as strongly. (D) heated — wrong tone.

Test the second blank: Evasion creates the impression of concealment, so "reinforced" fits. (A) confirmed.

Answer: (A).

The flow test. Read the completed sentence aloud (mentally). If it sounds natural and the meaning is coherent end-to-end, the option is likely correct. If it reads stilted or contradictory, eliminate.

Module 05 · Connectors & Transitions

The signal
vocabulary.

A small set of connective words and phrases does almost all the heavy lifting in paragraph coherence. Memorise the set; reading speed for VA improves by a factor of two.

Beneath every coherent paragraph runs a small lattice of transition words — "however", "moreover", "for example", "in contrast", "thus" — that signal the relationship between adjacent sentences. The skilled VA reader sees these signals first, and reads the surrounding content second. This module enumerates the signals and groups them by function.

5.1The six functional families

FunctionSignal wordsWhat follows
Contrasthowever, but, yet, nevertheless, on the contrary, in contrast, by comparisonAn opposing or qualifying claim
Continuationmoreover, furthermore, in addition, also, additionally, what is moreMore evidence for the same claim
Examplefor example, for instance, consider, as in the case ofA specific illustration of a general claim
Consequencethus, hence, therefore, consequently, as a result, accordinglyA conclusion drawn from the preceding
Concessionadmittedly, to be sure, granted, of course, no doubtA point granted to opponents (often followed by "but")
Restatementthat is, in other words, namely, i.e., put differentlyA clarification of the immediately preceding

5.2The contrast-pivot pattern

"However", "but", and "yet" are the highest-information connectives. A sentence that begins with one of these is signalling that the author's actual position differs from what was just said. The text just before the pivot is usually a setup; the text just after is the author's real claim.

The reversal mnemonic. When you see "however" at the start of a sentence, mentally underline the next 15 words. The author is about to say what they really think.

5.3The concession-pivot pattern

"Admittedly", "to be sure", "granted" — these flag a point the author is conceding to the other side. The concession is almost always followed within one or two sentences by a "but" or "however" that re-asserts the author's main claim. Skilled CAT readers recognise the concession as a two-step structure: "I admit X" + "but actually Y". The author still believes Y.

5.4Connectors in para-jumbles

For para-jumble work, the connector words tell you exactly which sentences can and cannot follow which. A sentence starting with "moreover" requires a preceding sentence stating the claim being extended. A sentence starting with "for instance" requires a preceding general claim. Tracking these constraints is faster than tracking content.

5.5The connectors that are not what they seem

A few connective words are commonly misread:

  • "While" — when at the start of a sentence, it usually signals concession ("While X is true, Y is also true"), not simultaneity.
  • "Since" — usually causal ("since X, Y"), not temporal.
  • "As" — can mean "because", "while", or "in the role of" depending on context. Disambiguate using the surrounding clauses.

Module 06 · Critical Reasoning

The argument-evaluation
question.

A short passage states an argument. The question asks what would strengthen, weaken, or follow from it. The skill is identifying the gap between the argument's evidence and its conclusion.

Critical reasoning questions are rare in the post-2015 CAT but recur in shadow papers and OMET (other management entrance tests). They give you a short argument and ask one of: what would weaken it, what would strengthen it, what is the assumption it makes, what can be inferred from it. The skill is essentially analytical: separate the evidence from the conclusion, identify the unstated link between them.

6.1The argument structure

Any argument has three parts Evidence: what the argument cites as factual.
Assumption: the unstated bridge between evidence and conclusion.
Conclusion: what the argument claims follows from the evidence.

The assumption is almost always the weakest part of the argument. Find it, and you can either strengthen the argument (by confirming the assumption) or weaken it (by undermining the assumption).

6.2The four question types

Question typeWhat it asksStrategy
WeakenWhich option makes the argument weaker?Undermine the assumption
StrengthenWhich option makes the argument stronger?Confirm the assumption
AssumptionWhat does the argument assume?Find the bridge between evidence and conclusion
InferenceWhat follows from the argument?What must be true if the argument is true?

6.3Worked weaken example

Argument: "Students who attend coaching classes for the CAT examination achieve, on average, higher percentiles than those who do not. The coaching classes must therefore be the cause of this performance difference."

Evidence: Coaching-class students have higher CAT percentiles.

Conclusion: Coaching classes cause the performance difference.

Assumption: The two groups (coached and uncoached) are otherwise comparable — same baseline ability, same study time, same motivation.

Question: Which option most weakens the argument?

  • (A) Students who self-study without coaching typically spend fewer hours preparing.
  • (B) Coaching classes have become more expensive over the past decade.
  • (C) Students who can afford coaching classes generally come from educational backgrounds that already place them above average academically.
  • (D) The CAT examination is conducted twice a year in some years.

Analysis: The assumption is that the two groups are comparable. (C) directly attacks this — if coached students start with an academic advantage, the percentile difference may reflect that advantage rather than the coaching itself. This is a classic "alternate cause" weaken.

(A) actually strengthens or supports the argument (fewer hours → lower performance). (B) is irrelevant. (D) is irrelevant.

Answer: (C).

The alternate-cause habit. Most CAT critical-reasoning weakeners take the form "X may not have caused Y; some third factor Z may have caused both X and Y". Train yourself to ask, for any cited correlation, "what else could explain this?"

Module 07 · Tips & Traps

Ten habits
that move the needle.

Most VA marks are lost not to ignorance but to haste. The fixes below are not new techniques; they are corrections to small habits that compound across eight questions.

Verbal Ability rewards a slightly slower reader. Each of the four sub-types asks you to do one specific thing well — find the opener, compress the argument, hear the odd note, predict the destination — and each one breaks down in a slightly different way. The tips below are organised loosely by sub-type, with a final cluster on test-day discipline.

TIP 01 · Para-jumble
Read the opener candidates aloud in your head.

An opener should feel like a paragraph beginning — neutral, topic-introducing, with no backward gestures. If a sentence sounds like an answer rather than a question, it is almost certainly not the opener.

TIP 02 · Para-jumble
The "this/that" trap.

A sentence beginning with "this", "that", "such", or "these" must follow a sentence that contains the noun being referred to. The reference is always backward, never forward. Use this to lock at least one pair.

TIP 03 · Para-jumble
Trust the "however"-pair, distrust the rest.

"However", "but", "yet", "nevertheless" reliably attach a sentence to the one immediately before it, since they contradict that previous claim. "Moreover" and "furthermore" are weaker bonds — they can sometimes sit two sentences after their referent.

TIP 04 · Para-summary
Write your own one-line summary before reading options.

This is the single highest-yield habit in VA. Cover the options with your hand. Read the paragraph. State its argument in one sentence in your head. Then match that sentence to the closest option. It removes most temptation.

TIP 05 · Para-summary
Reject scope drift, not detail loss.

A good summary must lose detail. What it cannot do is change the scope — narrow what the paragraph claimed universally, broaden what was qualified, or shift from "is" to "should". Detail loss is fine; scope drift is wrong.

TIP 06 · Odd Sentence Out
Find the theme before you hunt the odd one.

The four legitimate sentences share something — a topic, a thesis, a single example being elaborated. Name that shared thing in one phrase. The odd sentence is the one your phrase does not cover, even if its content seems related.

TIP 07 · Odd Sentence Out
Beware the plausible cousin.

CAT's favourite OSO trap is a sentence that is true, well-written, on the same broad topic, but at a different level of abstraction or arguing the opposite stance. "Related" is not "belongs". Test each sentence against the specific argument, not the general topic.

TIP 08 · Sentence Completion
Predict the ending before you peek.

Read the stem with the final sentence missing and finish it in your own words. Then pick the option that matches your prediction. Options that go in a direction you did not predict are almost always wrong — even when they are factually plausible.

TIP 09 · Test-day
Skip and return, ruthlessly.

VA questions have no negative marking only when they are TITA — and most are. But the time cost is real. If a para-jumble has resisted you for 90 seconds, mark it, move on, return at the end. The next question is almost always cheaper.

TIP 10 · Test-day
For TITA, attempt only when you have eliminated to one.

A wrong TITA answer is zero, the same as no attempt. So a 50-50 guess is exactly worth zero in expectation. Only commit to an answer when you have eliminated to a unique sequence or option — otherwise leave it blank.

7.1The five most common VA traps

Trap atlas The reverser: a summary option that flips the paragraph's stance ("the author argues against X" when the paragraph argues for X).
The narrower: an option that captures only one example from the paragraph, missing the general claim.
The broadener: an option that promotes a qualified claim into a universal one — drop the "often" or "sometimes" and the option is wrong.
The intruder: in OSO, a sentence on the same topic but with an unrelated thesis.
The deflector: a sentence-completion option that resolves a different question from the one the paragraph was actually setting up.

7.2What to do when you are stuck

If a para-jumble has resisted you, do this: identify the last sentence first. The closer is often the easiest to spot — it summarises, concludes, or projects forward. Once you have the closer, work backward; the second-to-last sentence usually sets up the closer's claim.

If a para-summary feels like a coin flip between two options, ask: which option's wording could the author have written, given the tone of the paragraph? The one whose register matches is almost always correct.

If an OSO has you stuck, name what the four legitimate sentences are doing. Are they all describing causes of something? All listing instances? All arguing for a thesis? The impostor is doing something subtly different — describing an effect when others describe causes, or generalising when others stay specific.

The discipline. Verbal Ability is won not by knowing more but by being patient with eight short paragraphs. Most candidates lose marks here by reading too fast and committing too soon. Slow your eye, name the argument, then choose.

Module 08 · Problem Bank

Twenty-five VA puzzles
across all four formats.

Ten para-jumbles, eight para-summaries, four odd-sentence-out, three sentence-completions. Each carries a full reasoning trace — opener, mandatory pairs, closer, or option-by-option elimination as the format demands.

Work each problem before opening the solution. Restate the paragraph's argument in your own words first; only then commit. For TITA items, the discipline is unforgiving — a wrong answer is the same as a blank, so do not commit until you can defend the sequence sentence by sentence.

8.1Para-jumbles · ten of them

Problem 1PJ · Easy
Para-jumble · 4 sentences

Arrange the following sentences in the most logical order:

1
However, recent neuroimaging studies suggest that this division is far less clean than the textbooks imply.
2
For most of the twentieth century, neuroscientists drew a firm line between the brain's "emotional" and "rational" circuits.
3
The two systems, it turns out, are densely interlinked, and what we call a "rational" judgement almost always carries an affective tag.
4
The amygdala was assigned the first job, the prefrontal cortex the second.

Type your answer: _ _ _ _

Step 1 — openerSentence 2 introduces the topic ("for most of the twentieth century, neuroscientists…") with no back-reference. 1 starts with "However" — disqualified. 3 starts with "The two systems" — refers to two systems already introduced. 4 mentions "the amygdala was assigned the first job" — "first job" implies a prior reference. Opener = 2.
Step 2 — mandatory pair4 mentions "the first job" and "the second" — referring back to the two functions ("emotional" and "rational") just named in 2. 2 → 4 is locked.
Step 3 — contrast1 begins "However, recent neuroimaging studies suggest that this division is far less clean…" — contradicting the firm line drawn in 2 and 4. So 1 follows 4. 4 → 1.
Step 4 — closer3 explains the consequence of 1 ("the two systems are densely interlinked"). 1 → 3.
Answer2 4 1 3 · classic "old consensus → details → however → consequence" arc.
Problem 2PJ · Medium
Para-jumble · 4 sentences

Arrange the following sentences in the most logical order:

1
Such a view, however, sits uncomfortably with the actual evidence — most languages have died not through replacement but through abandonment.
2
The standard story of language death is one of conquest: a politically dominant tongue rolls over its weaker neighbours and erases them.
3
Speakers stop transmitting their inherited language to their children, usually because the surrounding economic ecosystem makes another language more rewarding.
4
It is a slow withdrawal, not a sudden conquest, that depopulates a language.

Type your answer: _ _ _ _

Step 1 — opener2 introduces "the standard story of language death" without back-reference. 1 ("Such a view, however") — disqualified, refers backward. 3 ("Speakers stop transmitting…") — middle elaboration. 4 ("It is a slow withdrawal…") — sounds like a closer summarising. Opener = 2.
Step 2 — contrast pair1 says "Such a view, however, sits uncomfortably…" — directly responding to the "standard story" in 2. 2 → 1.
Step 3 — explain mechanism3 explains how abandonment happens (the mechanism behind 1's claim). 1 → 3.
Step 4 — closer4 generalises the mechanism into a closing line. 3 → 4.
Answer2 1 3 4 · standard story → counter-claim → mechanism → punchline closer.
Problem 3PJ · Medium
Para-jumble · 5 sentences

Arrange the following sentences in the most logical order:

1
In practice, however, the boundary between the two has dissolved almost entirely.
2
Documentary photography was, for most of the twentieth century, distinguished from art photography by a single test: was the image taken to record, or was it taken to express?
3
Curators routinely hang documentary work in galleries, and photographers who think of themselves as artists frequently embed themselves in newsrooms.
4
A recording, by this older logic, belonged in an archive; an expression belonged in a frame.
5
The result is that the older taxonomy survives mostly as a habit of speech rather than a description of practice.

Type your answer: _ _ _ _ _

Step 1 — opener2 introduces the topic and "the single test" — clean opener. 1 ("In practice, however…") — disqualified. 3 ("Curators routinely…") — example, must follow a claim. 4 ("by this older logic") — refers to a logic just stated. 5 ("The result is…") — summative closer. Opener = 2.

  • has periodically dominated the publishing market, with fashions shifting roughly every generation.
  • is the preferred approach of major literary translators today.
  • embodies a different theory of what reading is for, and the choice between them is therefore not just stylistic but ethical.
  • requires translators to be fluent in both the source and target languages.
Step 1 — predictThe paragraph has set up two schools as genuinely different approaches, each defensible — "neither approach is wrong in principle, but each ___". The expected continuation should say what each approach does that distinguishes it at the level of values or principles — not just market fashion or skill requirements.
Step 2 — eliminate(A) is a historical-market claim, not a principled distinction. (B) contradicts the paragraph (it does not claim one approach dominates). (D) is a generic translator skill, unrelated to the school distinction.
Step 3 — confirm(C) elevates the distinction from style to ethics ("different theory of what reading is for") — the natural deepening of "neither is wrong in principle, but each…". Predicted direction.
Answer(C)

The one-page VA
cheat sheet.

Everything in this Codex compressed into the table you would glance at over coffee on the morning of the exam. Use it as a final scan, not as a substitute for the modules.

9.1The four sub-types — what each tests

Sub-typeWhat you are givenWhat is being measuredFormat
Para-jumble 4 or 5 sentences from a coherent paragraph, in random order. Can you reconstruct the paragraph's flow using opener cues, mandatory pairs, and closer cues? TITA
Para-summary A 3–5 sentence paragraph and four candidate summaries. Can you separate the main claim from supporting detail, and reject summaries that distort scope, tone, or stance? MCQ (4 options)
Odd-sentence-out 5 sentences, four of which belong to a coherent paragraph; one is the impostor. Can you hear the theme and detect the sentence whose topic, abstraction, or tone is subtly off? TITA
Sentence-completion A paragraph with its final (or initial) sentence missing. Can you predict the paragraph's destination and reject options that turn left when the text was going straight? MCQ (4 options)

9.2Opener disqualifiers

Sentence begins with…Why it cannot be the opener
This / That / Such / TheseRefers backward to a noun in a previous sentence.
However / But / Yet / NeverthelessContradicts a position already stated.
Moreover / Furthermore / Also / AdditionallyExtends a claim already made.
Consequently / Thus / Hence / ThereforeConcludes from a previous step.
He / She / It / They (without a noun antecedent)Pronoun refers back to a previously named entity.
In contrast / By comparisonCompares to something already on the table.
For example / For instanceIllustrates a claim already made.

9.3The five most common para-summary traps

TrapWhat it doesHow to catch it
The reverserFlips the author's stance.Check whether the option says for what the paragraph said against, or vice versa.
The narrowerCaptures one detail or example, drops the general claim.Ask: does this option cover the paragraph's full scope, or only one paragraph-half?
The broadenerDrops a qualifier ("often", "in some cases") and turns a calibrated claim into a universal one.Compare option wording to paragraph wording — look for missing hedges.
The adderImports a claim, recommendation, or comparison the paragraph never made.If a key noun in the option is absent from the paragraph, the option is wrong.
The deflectorResolves a question different from the one the paragraph was asking.State the paragraph's central question first; reject options that answer a different one.

9.4Connective vocabulary — what they signal

ConnectiveWhat it tells you about the next sentence
However / But / YetContradicts or qualifies the previous claim.

For example / For instance / In particularIllustrates the previous claim.

9.5Test-day discipline

Three rules for VA on test day 1. Read the paragraph once for argument before reading the options. Name the argument in one sentence.
2. For TITA items, commit only when you have eliminated to a single sequence or option. A 50-50 guess is worth zero in expectation — the same as a blank.
3. Skip-and-return for any question that has resisted you past 90 seconds. The next VA puzzle is almost always cheaper than the one you are stuck on.

9.6The single most useful habit

Cover the options. Read the paragraph. State the argument. Then look.

This habit — refusing to be influenced by the options before you have understood the paragraph — is what separates a candidate scoring 80th percentile in VA from one scoring 95th. The options are designed to be plausible. Your own one-sentence summary, formed before the options arrive, is your only reliable defence.
