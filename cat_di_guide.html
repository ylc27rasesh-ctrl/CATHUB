Module 00 · Orientation

The section that rewards
readers, not solvers.

Data Interpretation is a reading test wearing a math costume. This codex teaches the reading first, the math second, and the set-selection instinct that decides scores most of all.

Data Interpretation sits inside the second section of the CAT, paired with Logical Reasoning under the combined heading LRDI. The section runs forty minutes and contains four sets — two pure DI, two pure LR, or some blend of the two depending on the year. Every set has four to six sub-questions. The block is worth roughly twenty marks of a possible sixty-six, and it is the single biggest score-spread between the 95th-percentile candidate and the 99th-percentile candidate. More than any other section of the CAT, DI is decided by which sets you attempt, not by how cleverly you attempt them.

Most students enter their first CAT mock and discover something disorienting: the math here is trivial. There are no algebraic gymnastics, no obscure formulas, no geometry. The numbers add and divide. What is hard is the reading — extracting from a five-line caselet exactly which constraint matters first, building a working table on rough paper, and then mining that table four or five times in succession. This codex is built around that single, unfamiliar skill.

0.1How this codex differs from the others

The seven Quant codices in this series each followed the same shape: nine modules of concept, with a problem bank of twenty-five individual questions. Data Interpretation cannot be taught that way. There is no topic here in the sense that Percentages is a topic. There are only sets — and each set is a self-contained world with its own table, its own rules, and its own sub-questions. Reading a set is the skill; solving the math inside it is the easy part.

So this codex is structured differently. Modules 1 through 7 teach the underlying skills — how a CAT DI set is built, how to triage in the first ninety seconds, which archetypes to expect, which traps to avoid. Module 8 is the spine of the book: twelve real CAT sets from 2017 to 2024, in full, with every sub-question answered and every reasoning step shown. The goal is not to memorise these sets, but to train the eye to recognise patterns. Six of these sets are pure DI; six are the logic-driven hybrid sets that have dominated CAT since 2018.

Teaching philosophy. The CAT does not test data interpretation. It tests data extraction. The student who can read a table once, build a clean mental model, and never look at the table again wins this section. The student who keeps re-reading the stem loses it.

0.2What "doing well" looks like

A 95th-percentile DI score is roughly two-and-a-half full sets attempted with high accuracy — about ten correct sub-questions out of twenty. A 99th-percentile score is three full sets cleanly attempted. The fourth set, if attempted at all, almost always destroys timing for everyone. The single most important strategic skill in this section, taught in Module 7, is set selection: deciding in the first ninety seconds of reading which two or three sets to commit to, and walking away from the others with no guilt.

Diagnostic for any new batch. Hand out the CAT 2023 Slot 2 DI set on game scoring (it is reproduced in Module 8 as Set 9). Give twelve minutes. The students who finish four out of five sub-questions correctly are 99-percentile candidates. The students who finish two out of five but show a clean table are 95-percentile material with training. The students who never built a table at all need to spend two weeks on Modules 2 and 3 before doing anything else.

0.3A note on what we mean by "real CAT sets"

The IIMs do not release CAT papers officially. The sets reproduced in Module 8 are the consensus reconstructions that appear across coaching institute archives — they reflect the actual sets students attempted on the day, with the wording cleaned for pedagogy and the source year clearly labelled. The years span 2017 through 2024 so that students see the drift in question style: from data-heavy table-and-bar formats (2017–2019) to the logic-puzzle hybrid style (2020–2024) that now dominates.

Module 01 · Anatomy

What a CAT DI set
is made of.

Every set in the last seven years can be decomposed into three layers — stem, structure, and questions. Once a student sees the layers, the section stops feeling unfamiliar.

A CAT DI set is a small machine with three parts. The stem is the prose at the top — usually four to ten lines that introduce the scenario and lay down the rules. The structure is the data presentation — sometimes a table, sometimes a chart, sometimes a caselet with no visual at all. The questions follow, typically four to six of them, each asking the student to extract or compute something from the structure under the rules of the stem.

1.1Layer one — the stem

The stem is where examiners hide most of the difficulty. Read carelessly, it costs you the entire set. Read carefully, and the rest is arithmetic. A typical CAT 2023 stem might read:

"In a chess tournament, eight players participate in a round-robin format. Each player plays every other player exactly once. A win earns 2 points, a draw earns 1 point to each player, and a loss earns 0. At the end of the tournament, no two players have the same number of points. The following partial information is known…"

That single paragraph carries five distinct rules: round-robin format (so each player plays exactly seven games, giving 28 total games and 56 distributed points), the scoring system (2/1/0), the uniqueness condition (points are all distinct), the player count (eight), and an implicit constraint that since 56 points are distributed across 8 distinct integer ranks, the only way to do it is a tight near-arithmetic-progression. The student who notices the 56-point total in the first read has done eighty percent of the work before seeing the first sub-question.

The stem rule. Read the stem twice before you look at the table. Underline every number. Circle every word that constrains a quantity — exactly, at most, distinct, each, some. CAT setters never write a word in a stem by accident.

1.2Layer two — the structure

The structure is what the stem points to: a table of values, a chart with bars, a pie graph, a network of nodes, or — increasingly — a partial table with deliberate blanks the student must fill in. CAT structures fall into about seven recognisable shapes, and each shape signals a slightly different solution discipline:

A · 2017
Pure table

Rows × columns of values, totals on margins. Read the totals first.

B · 2018
Bar / line graph

Visual where numbers must be read off axes. Estimate, don't measure.

C · 2019

D · 2020+

Some cells filled, others blank. The blanks are where the question lives.

E · 2021+

All information in prose. The student must construct the table themselves.

F · 2022+

G · 2023+

Players, matches, scoring rules. Pure logic with a numeric skin.

H · 2024

Three-set or four-set diagrams with overlap counts.

The eight shapes are not equally distributed. Since 2020, archetypes D, E, F, and G together account for roughly three out of four CAT DI sets. The classical chart-reading set has become the minority. Module 4 onwards focuses on these dominant archetypes.

1.3Layer three — the questions

The questions in a CAT DI set are deliberately graded. Sub-question 1 is almost always a direct read or a single-step computation, designed so any student who built the table correctly gets it. Sub-questions 2 and 3 require one or two further inferences. Sub-question 4 (and 5, if present) often demand a counterfactual — "if a new player is added…", "in how many ways could the remaining cells be filled?" — and are where examiners separate the field.

Easy sub-questions
  • "What was the value in row X, column Y?"
  • "By how much did X exceed Y?"
  • "What is the ratio of A to B?"
Hard sub-questions
  • "How many distinct configurations are possible?"
  • "What is the minimum value of X consistent with all constraints?"
  • "If condition Z is added, which players are eliminated?"

1.4The 90-second triage

Every successful DI candidate develops a routine for the first ninety seconds of seeing a set. The routine has three steps:

The triage rule Read the stem once. Glance at the structure. Read the first sub-question. Now decide: do I commit, or do I move on? You may not return to a set you have moved past — the time penalty of switching is too high.

The trigger to walk away is not difficulty alone. It is the density of constraints in the stem versus the time you have. A set with two clean rules and a 4×5 table is almost always doable in eight minutes. A set with eight interlocking rules in a five-line caselet is, even for the strongest candidate, a fifteen-minute commitment — and fifteen minutes is more than a third of your section.

Module 02 · Method

The method
that solves every set.

A five-step procedure for any CAT DI set, taught with a worked example and then drilled across every archetype in the chapters that follow.

There is exactly one method for solving CAT DI sets and it works whether the structure is a table, a chart, a network, or a caselet. The method has five steps, each of which can be done in under a minute on a clean set. Most students naturally do steps 1, 4, and 5. Skipping steps 2 and 3 is what causes the gap between 85th and 99th percentile.

2.1The five-step method

CAT DI · The five-step method
1 · Read. Read the stem twice. Underline numbers, circle constraint words.
2 · Frame. Build a clean working table on rough paper. Mirror the structure.
3 · Fill. Apply each given constraint in order. Stop when the table is maximally filled.
4 · Mine. For each sub-question, look only at the filled table — not the stem.
5 · Verify. Sanity-check totals, ratios, and any conserved quantity.

2.2A worked walk-through

To make the method concrete, consider a stylised set in the CAT 2019 style:

Stem. A company sells four products — A, B, C, D — through three channels — Online, Retail, Wholesale. The total sales (in ₹ lakh) for each channel and each product are partially given:

FRAME · STEP 2 The grid is 3 × 4 with margins. Twelve interior cells, four already known. We need eight more.
FILL · STEP 3 Grand total = 400. So column D total = 400 − 90 − 110 − 100 = 100. Now D's column: Retail D = 30, so Online D + Wholesale D = 70. Online row: 30 + ? + 25 + ? = 120, so Online B + Online D = 65. Wholesale row: 20 + ? + 35 + ? = 130, so Wholesale B + Wholesale D = 75. Column B: ? + 45 + ? = 110, so Online B + Wholesale B = 65. Two equations, two unknowns each — solvable.

From those linear conditions: solving Online B and Wholesale B from OB + WB = 65 and the row equations gives the full filled table. The point of the worked example is not the answer; it is the order. Marginal totals are filled first, then any cell that becomes uniquely determined, then any cell that requires a one-step inference. By the time the student reads sub-question 1, the table is complete.

2.3What to write on rough paper

The most underrated skill in DI is rough-paper hygiene. The student who writes nothing loses track. The student who writes everything runs out of paper. The optimum is roughly this:

  • Top of page: a one-line summary of the stem rules. (e.g. "8 players, RR, 2-1-0, distinct points")
  • Centre of page: the working table, redrawn cleanly, large enough to write inferences in. Never work inside the printed/displayed table.
  • Right margin: running totals and any derived equation.
  • Bottom of page: a list of "candidates" for each unknown — values still consistent with constraints. Cross them off as the set progresses.

The redraw rule. Always copy the table by hand. The act of copying forces you to read every cell. Students who try to work directly off the screen miss data nine times out of ten — and the tenth time they miss a constraint hidden in the stem.

2.4The "stop filling" signal

A common mistake is to keep grinding at a partial table until every cell is determined. Sometimes the cells are not determinable from the stem alone — they require a sub-question's additional information to lock down. The signal to stop filling is when, after applying every constraint, you cannot uniquely determine any additional cell. At that point, move to sub-question 1; it will almost certainly hand you the next piece of information.

The information ladder. CAT setters design DI sets so that the stem gives you 70% of the information, and each subsequent sub-question can add the rest. Read the questions in order — they are sequenced for cumulative discovery.

Module 03 · Classical archetypes

Tables, bars,
and pies.

The three classical DI structures that dominated CAT papers between 2008 and 2019. Less common now, but still worth roughly one set out of four.

For more than a decade, DI on the CAT meant exactly this: a table of numbers, a bar chart you read off, or a pair of pie charts that linked through a common total. These sets still appear — usually one per paper since 2020 — and they are the highest-percentage sets to attempt. The arithmetic is heavier than logic-DI, but the table is given, the rules are minimal, and the difficulty is almost entirely in fast reading and clean computation. A student who can finish two table-and-chart sets in twenty minutes has already secured a 95th-percentile DI score.

3.1Pure tables · the reading discipline

A pure table set gives you all data upfront. The rows and columns are labelled, the unit is stated, and there are usually margin totals. The trap is not the math — it is the misread. CAT setters write rows like "Imports from country X (in thousand metric tons)" right next to a column header reading "Year (in CY)", and students who confuse calendar year for fiscal year lose three sub-questions in succession.

The unit trap. Always circle the unit in the table caption before reading any value. CAT 2018 famously had a table where exports were in lakh kilograms and imports were in tonnes — the conversion factor 10 trips up about a third of all candidates each year.

Working drill — table-reading

Drill 3.1

Given a 4-row, 5-column table with all totals shown: how fast can you compute (a) the row whose average is highest, (b) the column whose share of the grand total exceeds 30%, and (c) the cell whose value most exceeds its row-mean? Target: under sixty seconds.

This is the most useful drill in this module. Run it on every CAT 2014–2019 paper you can find.

3.2Bar charts · estimation over precision

Bar charts on the CAT come in three flavours: single-variable bars (each bar a quantity), stacked bars (each bar composed of two or three coloured segments), and grouped bars (two or three adjacent bars per category, like quarterly revenue by year). The single most important skill in reading bars is estimation. The answer options are always spaced far enough apart that an estimate to the nearest 5% of the maximum is sufficient.

Never measure pixels. A bar that reaches three-quarters of the way to the gridline labelled 200 is worth 150, not 148. The CAT setter chose 150. Estimate and move.

Reading stacked bars

Stacked bars are notoriously misread. The trick is to remember that each colour-segment's height is measured from the bottom of that segment, not from zero. A stack reading 0–40 (blue) then 40–110 (red) means the blue is 40 and the red is 70, not 110. CAT 2017 had a stacked-bar set where the question asked which year saw the largest year-on-year growth in the middle segment — and a third of candidates compared the cumulative heights instead of the segment heights.

3.3Pie charts · the linker

Pie charts almost never appear alone on the CAT. They appear in pairs, with a stated link variable that lets you cross from one chart to the other. A common construction: Pie 1 shows the breakup of total revenue across six product lines; Pie 2 shows the breakup of profit across the same six lines. The link is the profit margin per line, which requires computing both shares from the absolute total.

Pie chart conversion Percentage on pie → degree on circle: multiply by 3.6. (25% = 90°)
Degree on pie → percentage: divide by 3.6. (72° = 20%)

The trap with two-pie sets is forgetting that a slice's share may be the same in two pies even when the absolute value is wildly different — because the totals behind each pie are different. Always anchor each pie to its declared total before doing any cross-comparison.

3.4What CAT does with combinations

The hardest table-and-chart sets pair structures: a table that gives you absolute values combined with a bar chart that gives you growth rates, or a pie chart that gives you composition combined with a line chart that gives you trend. The shared variable is your bridge. Find it in the first thirty seconds; everything else follows.

Easy combos
  • Table + single bar chart (same metric)
  • Pie + pie (linked by total)
  • Line + table (same time axis)
Hard combos
  • Absolute table + growth-rate chart
  • Pie + bar (different units)
  • Three or more linked structures

Module 04 · Modern archetypes

When the table
isn't given.

The caselet — a paragraph of prose with no chart — has become the defining CAT DI archetype since 2020. The student who builds the table best wins the set.

A caselet is a DI set without a chart. All the information sits in prose: a paragraph of three to ten lines, sometimes with a list of bullet-point constraints, sometimes with embedded sub-conditions. The student must construct the data structure themselves before any sub-question can be touched. Caselets are now the single most common CAT DI archetype, present in almost every paper since 2020. They reward students who think in tables.

4.1Why caselets dominate now

The shift from chart-based DI to caselet-based DI tracks the changing nature of the CAT. The CAT once tested business-school readiness through arithmetic; it now tests it through structured reasoning. A caselet asks: given imperfect, half-stated information, can you build the right mental model? That is exactly the skill an analyst uses on the first day of a consulting case. The CAT setters know this. So do the IIMs.

2020 inflection. The CAT 2020 paper had three caselets out of four DI sets — the first paper in CAT history where pure-chart DI was the minority. Every subsequent year has followed the same pattern.

4.2How to read a caselet

Caselets are read in three passes:

PASS 1 · INVENTORY Read the entire caselet without trying to solve anything. Count the entities (people, products, days), count the attributes (scores, prices, categories), and note any totals or limits.
PASS 2 · FRAME Draw the table. Rows = entities, columns = attributes. Leave every cell blank.
PASS 3 · FILL Re-read the caselet sentence by sentence. Each sentence translates to one fill in the table — or a constraint between cells. Process in the order given.

Most students try to solve while reading. That is the wrong order. Solving without framing is how students miss the constraint hidden in a clause like "no two of the four products had the same price" — a single condition that, in CAT 2022, eliminated three of the five answer options on its own.

4.3The constraint vocabulary

Every CAT caselet uses a small, repeated vocabulary of constraint words. Learning to translate each one into a table-rule is the most leveraged skill in this module.

4.4When the caselet has no table

Occasionally a CAT caselet describes a situation that doesn't fit a table — a sequence of events, a network of relationships, or a scheduling problem. In those cases the structure should match the data: a timeline for sequences, an arrow diagram for relationships, a Gantt chart for schedules. The rule is the same as before: build the right scaffold, then mine it for answers.

The right scaffold. Table for attributes-of-entities. Sequence diagram for ordered events. Network for relationships. Venn for set overlaps. Match the scaffold to the data, and the questions almost answer themselves.

4.5The "logical-DI" hybrid

Since CAT 2018, examiners have begun mixing pure DI with pure LR within the same set. These are the "logical-DI" hybrids: a set that looks like DI (with numbers, totals, constraints) but is really an LR puzzle in disguise (with conditions, eliminations, and case analysis). The dead giveaway is the sub-question that asks "In how many ways can the table be completed?" — a question that wouldn't appear in a pure DI set, because pure DI sets have unique answers.

Logical-DI sets are best treated as LR sets. They reward case analysis: enumerate possibilities, eliminate by constraint, and count what survives. The classical DI instinct of "compute and move" fails on these. Patience and careful enumeration win.

Module 05 · Set-and-graph archetypes

Sets, graphs,
and shortest paths.

Three structural cousins that share one underlying idea — partitioning and counting under constraint. CAT 2021 onward has leaned heavily on these.

Venn diagrams, network problems, and routing puzzles all share a structural similarity: they ask the student to count, allocate, or route under constraint, with information delivered as relationships rather than as a table of values. Together they account for roughly one in three modern CAT DI sets, and they reward students who are willing to draw rather than tabulate.

5.1Venn diagrams · the standard formulas

Venn problems on the CAT come in two-set, three-set, and (rarely) four-set varieties. The two-set case is trivial. The three-set case is the workhorse and is examined almost every year. The four-set case is rare but has appeared (CAT 2019) — it requires the inclusion-exclusion principle in full generality.

Three-set inclusion-exclusion |A ∪ B ∪ C| = |A| + |B| + |C| − |A ∩ B| − |A ∩ C| − |B ∩ C| + |A ∩ B ∩ C|

The CAT does not just ask "find |A ∪ B ∪ C|". It asks more interesting questions: "find the number of students who took exactly two subjects", "find the number who took none", "find the maximum possible value of |A ∩ B ∩ C| consistent with the given totals". The trick is to label the seven Venn regions (a three-set diagram has seven non-empty regions plus a "neither" region) and write one equation per known quantity.

The "exactly k" decoder. For three sets, "exactly 1" = (A only) + (B only) + (C only). "Exactly 2" = sum of three pairwise intersections − 3 × (triple intersection). "All three" = the triple intersection. Setting up these counts is half the battle.

5.2The "maximum / minimum overlap" twist

The CAT loves to ask: "What is the maximum possible value of |A ∩ B ∩ C|?" or "What is the minimum number of students who took all three subjects?" These are not formula problems. They are bounding problems. The constraints — total students, individual subject totals, pairwise overlaps — must all be satisfied, and the student finds the extreme value of the triple overlap by pushing the other regions to their limits.

The pigeonhole intuition. Maximum triple overlap is bounded by the smallest of the three set sizes. Minimum triple overlap arises when the sets cover as wide an area as possible — given by inclusion-exclusion when |A ∪ B ∪ C| is at its maximum (often the total population).

5.3Networks · nodes, edges, capacities

Network DI sets give the student a graph: a few nodes (cities, computers, people) connected by edges (roads, links, friendships) sometimes carrying weights (distances, costs, capacities). The questions ask about paths, totals, or capacities. CAT 2019 introduced this archetype with a famous "five cities, six roads, distances given" set; it has reappeared in subtler forms since.

The two skills are: (a) reading the graph onto paper — always redraw, never solve from the printed diagram, and (b) systematically enumerating paths. For four-node graphs there are usually six or fewer simple paths; for five-node graphs, twelve or fewer. Brute enumeration beats clever algorithms at CAT scale.

5.4Routing · the travelling salesman shadow

Routing problems are network problems with a constraint that paths must visit certain nodes. The CAT never asks for an actual TSP solution (that would be intractable in three minutes), but it does ask: "What is the minimum total distance to visit all five cities starting from A?" with a graph small enough that all six possible orderings can be computed by hand.

Module 06 · Logic-heavy archetypes

Games, tournaments,
and scoring.

The marquee CAT DI archetype of the 2020s — pure logic in numeric clothing. Solving these sets requires patience, enumeration, and a love of constraint propagation.

A tournament-and-scoring set typically describes a small competition: six to ten players, a round-robin or knockout format, a scoring rule, and partial information about who won what. The student is asked to deduce the full result. These sets are pure logic dressed up as DI, and they are now the archetype most strongly associated with the high-percentile CAT score. They are also the archetype where unprepared students bleed the most time.

6.1The scoring-rule starting point

Every tournament set has a scoring rule. The student must compute, before reading any sub-question, the total points available in the tournament. For an n-player round robin:

Round-robin total points Number of matches = n(n−1)/2
Total points (any scoring rule that distributes a fixed total per match) = matches × total per match.
Example: 8 players, 2/1/0 scoring → 28 matches × 2 points = 56 total points.

This total is the single most powerful piece of information in the set. Sub-questions about averages, distributions, and rankings all return to it.

6.2The uniqueness condition

The most common stem-rule in tournament sets is "no two players had the same score". This condition is enormously constraining. With eight players and a 56-point total, distributed across eight distinct non-negative integers each at most 14 (which is 7 wins × 2 points), the possible sequences of distinct scores summing to 56 are highly limited. A student who lists those sequences before reading the sub-questions has the answer half-built already.

The point of the table is not the specific sequences. It is the discipline of enumerating. The student who, faced with "eight distinct integers summing to 56 each ≤ 14", reaches for a piece of paper and starts writing candidate sequences will solve the set. The student who doesn't, won't.

6.3Position vs points

Tournament sets often distinguish between a player's rank (1st, 2nd, 3rd…) and a player's points. The rank ordering is what the student sees first; the points are what determine the rank. A common sub-question is: "If Player X is ranked 3rd, what is the minimum number of wins X could have?" — this requires the student to think simultaneously about the ranking constraint (X's points must lie between rank-2's and rank-4's) and the win-count constraint (each win contributes 2 points, each draw 1).

The two-numbers trap. Players win games and earn points. CAT setters slip between the two registers without flagging it. "How many matches did X play?" is always n−1 in a round robin. "How many did X win?" depends on points and draws together. Read the question with care.

6.4Knockout tournaments

Knockout tournaments are easier than round-robins because the structure is fixed: an n-player single-elimination knockout has exactly n−1 matches, and each match has one winner and one loser. The interesting question is usually about the seeding — which players could possibly meet in which round given the bracket. CAT 2023 had a stylised tennis-knockout set with eight players seeded 1–8 where the question was: "In how many ways can players seeded 5 and 8 meet, given the standard bracket?"

Knockout heuristic. Two players in opposite halves can only meet in the final. Two players in the same quarter can only meet in the quarter-final or earlier. Memorising the bracket structure of a standard 8-team knockout saves three minutes on any seeding question.

6.5League-stage scoring with bonus / penalty

The most recent twist (CAT 2023, CAT 2024) adds non-trivial scoring rules: bonuses for winning by a margin, penalties for late finishes, half-points for ties. These are still tractable — the student just has to carefully maintain a side-table of bonuses earned. The math doesn't get harder, but the case enumeration grows.

Module 07 · Strategy

Selection, traps,
and the clock.

DI is decided as much by which sets you skip as by which you solve. The strategy module is the most important one for any student in the 80–95 percentile range trying to push higher.

Every CAT student arrives at this section having mastered, to some degree, the math and the logic. What separates a 90th-percentile DI score from a 99th-percentile DI score is not skill; it is judgement. Specifically, three judgements: which sets to attempt, when to abandon a set in progress, and which sub-questions inside a set to skip. This module collects the tactical wisdom that turns content knowledge into a score.

7.1Set selection · the first ninety seconds

Open the LRDI section. You have four sets in front of you, in some order. The first ninety seconds is for triage. Do not start solving. Instead:

  1. Read the stem of each set in twenty seconds. Note the archetype.
  2. Glance at the structure for each set — is the table given, or must you build one?
  3. Read sub-question 1 of each set. Is it a direct read, or does it require inference?
  4. Rank the four sets in your head by attemptability. Commit to the top two.
The 2-out-of-4 doctrine. Plan to attempt the best two sets first, in full. Then evaluate whether to attempt a third. The fourth set should be considered only if you have meaningful time left after a high-accuracy first three.

7.2The seven traps

TRAP 01
The Sunk-Cost Set

You've spent twelve minutes on a set and solved one sub-question. The fact that you've already invested twelve minutes is not a reason to invest more. Abandon and move.

TRAP 02
The Wrong Triage

The set that looks easy on first read often hides the worst trap. The set that looks intimidating often has a clean structure. Don't judge by appearance alone — read sub-question 1.

TRAP 03
The Unit Mismatch

Table says "thousand tonnes", question says "kilograms". A two-decimal-place miss costs you the entire set.

TRAP 04
The Implicit Constraint

"Each player plays at most twice" implies an upper bound, but most students read it as exactly twice. Constraint words are precise.

TRAP 05
The Cascading Error

You misread one cell on sub-question 1. Now sub-questions 2, 3, and 4 are all wrong even though your logic is right. Always verify the easy sub-questions first.

TRAP 06
The Phantom "All"

"In how many ways can the table be completed?" — students assume there's a unique completion. There often isn't, and the answer might be three or four valid completions.

TRAP 07
The Final Sub-Question

The hardest sub-question is usually #4 or #5. If it's eating more than two minutes and the rest of the set is solved, skip it. One sub-question is not worth the entire next set.

7.4What to do if you finish early

If you have eight or more minutes left after three sets, attempt the fourth — but only as a fish-for-easy-marks exercise. Read the first sub-question. If it's a direct read, solve it. Otherwise, leave the set. A single correct sub-question is worth three marks; a hasty incorrect attempt loses one. Pure expected-value tells you not to guess on this section.

The negative-marking calculation. CAT LRDI MCQs award +3 for correct, −1 for incorrect, 0 for non-attempt. A pure guess on a 4-option MCQ has expected value (3 × 0.25) + (−1 × 0.75) = 0. Guessing breaks even. Educated guessing — where you've eliminated two options — has positive expected value. Wild guessing on a set you haven't read does not.

7.5Mock-test discipline

Set-selection cannot be taught in theory. It is built only through mock tests. The recommended discipline: take a full LRDI section under timed conditions, then after the timer ends, redo the section without a time limit. Compare scores. If your timed score is below 60% of your untimed score, your weakness is set-selection, not content knowledge. Drill triage, not concepts.

9.5The seven traps

  • Sunk cost — time spent is not a reason to spend more.
  • Wrong triage — easy-looking sets often hide the worst trap.
  • Unit mismatch — circle the unit before reading any value.
  • Implicit constraint — read constraint words literally.
  • Cascading error — verify sub-Q 1 before going further.
  • Phantom "all" — uniqueness is not implied.
  • Final sub-Q — don't lose a set for one stubborn question.

9.6The mantra

DI is a reading test. The student who reads the stem once and builds the right scaffold has done eighty percent of the work. The math is trivial. Trust the method.
