Module 00 · Orientation

Why averages
is the quietest
scoring topic in QA.

A teacher's overview of why this chapter is short to teach, gentle to grade, and consistently four to eight questions of marks across every recent CAT. Where to spend time, where to skim, and how to position it alongside percentages.

Averages is the textbook example of a topic that students dismiss because the formula is one line, only to lose marks on it because every actual CAT question is about the formula in disguise. The arithmetic mean is, properly understood, not a single number but a statement about totals. CAT setters never test whether a student can compute (12 + 18 + 27)/3. They test whether the student can think in terms of total = average × count when the problem hands them an average and asks for a total — or hands them part of a total and asks for an average. The shift is small. The impact is enormous.

Averages is also the chapter where the volume of questions in recent CAT papers is rising. In CAT 2022, eight questions across the three slots touched averages directly or were weighted-average problems in costume. Even in years with fewer direct questions, averages appears inside data interpretation, mixtures, motion problems, and ratio chapters. Teach this topic well and a student gains marks in five other chapters too.

0.1How to use this codex in class

The nine modules are sequenced for a 6–8 hour block of teaching, shorter than the percentages block because the conceptual surface is smaller. Modules 1 and 2 lay the philosophical groundwork — the mean as a balancing point, not a calculation. Module 3 covers the entire family of "what happens to the average when one element changes" problems, which is CAT 2023 in a microcosm. Modules 4 and 5 are where the most marks live: weighted averages and alligation. Module 6 collects applied themes; Module 7 the tactical wisdom; Module 8 a graded problem bank; Module 9 the wall-poster summary.

Teaching philosophy. Insist from day one that the student translate every "average" into "total" before doing anything else. Average × count = total is the master identity. The student who reaches for this identity first will solve eighty percent of CAT averages questions in under ninety seconds.

0.2What the CAT actually asks

Scanning the 2017–2025 CAT papers, averages-based questions split cleanly into seven recurring archetypes:

  • Direct totals. "The average of a group is X; one element is replaced; find the new average." Solved by translating to totals and tracking the change.
  • Weighted averages and group merging. "Class A has average 60, Class B has average 80; combined average?" Two groups, two averages, two weights — alligation territory.
  • Conditional averages. "Average of the first n is A, average of the first n+1 is B, find the (n+1)th element." A standard pattern; the student should recognise it in one second.
  • Sub-group constraints. Like CAT 2022's bonus problem: averages of overlapping subsets of employees, all of which must be consistent.
  • Bounds and optimisation. Like CAT 2022's "six distinct natural numbers with two-end constraints"; the student must use the structure of the mean to bound the answer.
  • Average involving change. "Adding a person of weight w changes the average by Δ kg." The pivot variable in such problems is always number of people, and the trick is the small algebraic identity in Module 3.
  • Cricket / batting-average problems. Specific to averages with a not-out twist. Less common in CAT itself but very common in mocks and a great teaching vehicle for the conditional average.

Classroom diagnostic in two minutes. Ask: "The average age of 11 cricketers is 25. The coach (age 50) is included. What's the new average?" The fast student computes (11 × 25 + 50)/12 = 25/12 + 50/12 ≈ 27.08 in twenty seconds. The slow student stares. The struggler answers "37.5" by averaging 25 and 50 directly. Use the diagnostic to identify the third group; the codex is built to rescue them.

0.3What averages is not

Three quick clarifications worth airing on day one. First, "average" in CAT always means the arithmetic mean unless specified otherwise. Median and mode are tested rarely and only in DI sets. Second, the arithmetic mean of two quantities is not necessarily their best summary — but for CAT purposes, when a problem says "average", you compute arithmetic mean. Third, averages and percentages share a structural cousin called the weighted average; the alligation rule in Module 5 sits at the exact intersection of these two chapters. Students who do not yet have percentages fluent should pause and consolidate that chapter before pushing deep into Module 4 here.

Module 01 · Foundations

What an average
actually is.

Building the mental model from zero. Sum divided by count — yes. But more usefully, a balancing point. By the end of this module, students should never again reach for the calculator first.

1.1The literal definition

The arithmetic mean — the only average that matters for CAT — is the sum of all values divided by the count of values. The familiar one-liner:

The Definition Average = Sum / Count
For numbers a₁, a₂, …, aₙ:
Avg = (a₁ + a₂ + … + aₙ) / n

This is the form the student already knows. The shift this codex insists on is the rearranged form:

The Useful Form (memorise!) Sum = Average × Count
i.e. a₁ + a₂ + … + aₙ = n × Avg

Whenever a CAT problem hands you an average, write down the total before anything else. The total is the workable quantity. The average is an interpretation of the total. Reaching for the total first turns nine out of ten problems into a single algebraic line.

1.2The average as a balancing point

Here is the intuition that separates strong students from average ones. Picture the values on a number line. The average is the balance point: the spot where, if you put equal-mass beads at each value, the line balances. Every value above the mean contributes a positive deviation; every value below contributes a negative deviation; and these deviations cancel exactly.

The Balancing Property For values a₁, a₂, …, aₙ with mean M:
(a₁ − M) + (a₂ − M) + … + (aₙ − M) = 0
The sum of deviations from the mean is always zero.

This identity is more powerful than it looks. Many CAT problems give you partial information about deviations — "three numbers exceed the mean by 4, 7, and 11; the rest fall short of the mean by some total amount; find that total". The balancing property hands you the answer instantly: the total shortfall must be exactly 22.

The teaching analogy. Tell the class: the mean is the see-saw fulcrum. The students above-average and below-average are sitting on opposite sides. The product of their displacement and weight is the same on each side. This metaphor will carry you all the way to weighted averages in Module 4.

1.3The three quantities — and reading the question

Every averages problem has exactly three quantities in play: Sum, Count, and Average. The question gives you two; it asks for the third — or, more often, it gives you two of the three for one situation and two of the three for a related situation, and asks how something changes.

What you're given

"The average of 12 students is 65."
That gives you the sum: 12 × 65 = 780. Write this down immediately. Don't carry the average around; carry the sum.

What you need to find

If the question asks "what does the average become when…", you compute the new sum and new count, then divide. If it asks "what is the missing value", you compute the desired new sum and subtract what you have.

1.4A worked walkthrough to seal the module

Foundation example — the coach joins the team Easy

The average age of a cricket team of 11 players is 25 years. The coach, aged 50, is included in the squad. What is the new average age of the squad?

STEP 1 · Translate to total Sum of player ages = 11 × 25 = 275 years.
STEP 2 · Add the coach New sum = 275 + 50 = 325. New count = 12.
STEP 3 · Average back out New average = 325 / 12 = 27.08… ≈ 27 years 1 month.
Answer≈ 27.08 years

Teaching note: the wrong instinct is to "average" 25 and 50 to get 37.5. Drill the discipline of always going through the total. The reason the wrong answer feels right is that students intuit a midpoint, forgetting that the coach is one of twelve, not one of two.

· In-class drill ·

Round-robin, no calculator, ten seconds each. Express each as a sum first.

Average of 8 numbers is 12. Sum? · Average of 15 numbers is 20. Sum? · Average of 6 numbers is 30, average of next 4 is 50. Average of all 10?

Answers: 96 · 300 · 38.

Module 02 · Properties

Five properties
of the mean
that matter.

Five identities that — once memorised — let students short-circuit eighty percent of CAT averages questions without setting up any equations at all.

The arithmetic mean has a handful of crisp algebraic properties. Most coaching books list them but never explain why each one is useful. This module ties each property to a recurring CAT pattern, so the student learns the property in the same breath as the question type that exploits it.

2.1Property One — Shifting the data shifts the mean

Shift Property If each of a₁, a₂, …, aₙ has the same constant k added to it, the new mean is M + k.
If each has k subtracted, the new mean is M − k.

Useful when a CAT problem says "five years later the average age is…". You don't have to recompute. Each person is older by five years, so the mean is older by five years. Trivial in isolation, gold in combination with the next property.

2.2Property Two — Scaling the data scales the mean

Scale Property If each of a₁, a₂, …, aₙ is multiplied by a constant c, the new mean is c × M.

If a class's average salary doubles uniformly, the new average is double. If every score in an exam is scaled to be out of 50 instead of 100, the average halves. Combined with shift property, you get the affine rule: applying a transformation x → cx + k to every value sends the mean from M to cM + k.

2.3Property Three — The mean lies between min and max

Bound Property For any data set with minimum value m and maximum value M:
m ≤ Average ≤ M

Stronger version: the average equals the minimum only if all values are the minimum (similarly for the max). This property is the engine behind every "find the maximum/minimum possible average given constraints" question, of which CAT 2022 had a beautiful one (we solve it in Module 8 as Q21).

2.4Property Four — Adding the mean preserves the mean

Self-Preserving Property If you add a new value equal to the current mean M to a data set, the mean stays M.
Add a value greater than M, the mean rises. Add a value less than M, the mean falls.

This is intuitive but worth stating explicitly because of a subtle corollary used heavily in CAT problems:

The corollary. If a new value greater than the mean is added, the increase in the mean depends on how much the new value exceeds the old mean and on how many values there were already. Specifically: new mean − old mean = (new value − old mean) / new count. Memorise this. Module 3 makes it earn its keep.

2.5Property Five — The mean of consecutive integers

Symmetric Set Property The mean of any arithmetic progression is the average of its first and last terms:
M = (a₁ + aₙ) / 2
In particular: mean of {1, 2, …, n} = (n+1)/2. Mean of {2, 4, 6, …, 2n} = n+1.

Wherever the values in a CAT problem are evenly spaced — consecutive integers, an arithmetic sequence of ages, a sequence of multiples — this collapses the entire sum computation into one line. Use it.

2.6Worked applications of the properties

Property 5 in action Easy

Find the average of the first 30 multiples of 7.

METHOD The values are 7, 14, 21, …, 210 — an arithmetic progression. Average = (first + last)/2 = (7 + 210)/2 = 217/2 = 108.5.
Answer108.5
Property 4 in action — adding above the mean Easy

The average mark of 20 students is 72. A new student joins with a mark of 92. By how much does the average increase?

USE THE COROLLARY New mean − Old mean = (new value − old mean) / new count = (92 − 72)/21 = 20/21 ≈ 0.95.
AnswerAverage rises by ≈ 0.95 marks

Teaching note: the formula reflects intuition. The new student is 20 marks above the old mean. That 20-mark surplus is spread evenly across the 21 students now in the group. Hence each student's "share" of the surplus is 20/21. That's exactly how much the mean rises.

Module 03 · The Δ Engine

Replacement,
addition,
departure.

When the count stays fixed but a value is swapped. When the count grows. When the count shrinks. Three near-identical situations, three near-identical formulas — and a CAT setter's favourite playground.

3.1The master identity revisited

Every problem in this module is solvable in two steps: (1) compute the change in the sum; (2) compute the change in the count. Then divide. Nothing else. Let's formalise the three sub-cases.

3.2Sub-case A — Replacement (count unchanged)

An old value v_out is removed and a new value v_in takes its place. The count stays the same.

Replacement Identity Change in sum = v_in − v_out
Change in mean = (v_in − v_out) / n

If the average mark of 30 students is 70 and one student's score is corrected from 50 to 80, the sum rises by 30, and the new average is 70 + 30/30 = 71. One line.

3.3Sub-case B — Addition (count grows by one)

A new value v_new joins the group. The count goes from n to n + 1.

Addition Identity New mean = (n × M_old + v_new) / (n + 1)
Equivalently, new mean − old mean = (v_new − M_old) / (n + 1)

The second form is the gold one. It says: the change in mean equals the new value's distance from the old mean, divided by the new total count. This is the corollary we met in Module 2 — now it's the workhorse of an entire problem family.

3.4Sub-case C — Removal (count shrinks by one)

A value v_out leaves the group. The count goes from n to n − 1.

Removal Identity New mean = (n × M_old − v_out) / (n − 1)
Equivalently, new mean − old mean = (M_old − v_out) / (n − 1)

Same logic as addition, with the signs flipped. If the leaver was above the mean, removing them drags the mean down; if below, removing them lifts the mean up.

3.5The unified mental shortcut

One sentence to memorise. When a single element is swapped, added, or removed, the change in the mean equals the "out-of-line" distance of that element from the mean, divided by the final count of elements. The sign tracks direction; the magnitude tracks how far out of line the element was.

3.6The killer CAT pattern

CAT 2023 — D and E swap problem (adapted) Hard

If a person D joins a room of A, B and C, the average weight of the persons in the room reduces by x kg. Instead of D, if person E joins, the average weight increases by 2x kg. If E weighs 12 kg more than D, find the value of x.

  • 2
  • 0.5
  • 1
  • 1.5
STEP 1 · Set up Let the original mean of A, B, C be M. Sum of three = 3M.
When D joins: new mean = (3M + D)/4 = M − x. So 3M + D = 4M − 4x, giving D = M − 4x.
When E joins instead: new mean = (3M + E)/4 = M + 2x. So 3M + E = 4M + 8x, giving E = M + 8x.
STEP 2 · Use the weight gap E − D = (M + 8x) − (M − 4x) = 12x. We are told this equals 12. So 12x = 12 ⟹ x = 1.
AnswerOption C · 1

Teaching note: this is a perfect example of why translating averages into sums is non-negotiable. Trying to reason directly about "the mean changed by x" without writing down 3M + D = 4(M − x) leaves the student grasping at intuitions. Once written out, the algebra is a single line.

3.7The classic batting-average problem

Batting average — adapted classic Medium

A batsman has an average of 40 runs over 16 innings. In the 17th innings he scores a century. What does his average become?

SUMS Old sum = 16 × 40 = 640. New sum = 640 + 100 = 740. New average = 740/17 ≈ 43.53.
SHORTCUT The century is 60 above the old mean. Distributed over the new count of 17: 60/17 ≈ 3.53. So new average = 40 + 3.53 = 43.53.
Answer≈ 43.53 runs per innings
Batting average — the reverse direction Medium

A cricketer has played some innings with a certain average. In his next innings he scores 95 runs and his average rises from x to x + 5. After the innings, his total innings played is 12. What was his original average?

SETUP Before the 12th innings he had played 11 innings with average x. Sum before = 11x. After the 12th innings: 11x + 95 = 12(x + 5).
SOLVE 11x + 95 = 12x + 60 ⟹ 35 = x. So his original average was 35 runs.
AnswerOriginal average = 35 runs

Module 04 · Weighted Means

When the groups
have different
sizes.

Combining two or more groups, each with its own average and its own count. This is where most CAT averages questions actually live — and where alligation begins.

4.1Why the naïve answer is almost always wrong

Class A has 20 students with an average mark of 60. Class B has 30 students with an average of 80. What's the combined average? Untrained students answer "70" — the simple average of 60 and 80. That is wrong, because Class B has more students. The combined average must be pulled closer to 80.

The correct method: compute the totals, add them, divide by the combined count.

Weighted Average — Definition For two groups with counts n₁, n₂ and averages A₁, A₂:
Combined Average = (n₁A₁ + n₂A₂) / (n₁ + n₂)

Applied: (20 × 60 + 30 × 80)/(20 + 30) = (1200 + 2400)/50 = 3600/50 = 72. Not 70. The weighted average sits closer to 80 because Class B has more weight.

4.2The general formula

k-Group Weighted Average For k groups with counts n₁, n₂, …, n_k and averages A₁, A₂, …, A_k:
Avg = (Σ nᵢAᵢ) / (Σ nᵢ)

The denominator is the combined count. The numerator is the combined sum. Compute each piece, divide.

4.3The proportion-weight version

Sometimes a CAT problem doesn't tell you absolute counts — it gives you proportions. "Manufacturing employees are 20% of total staff; their average salary is X; non-manufacturing employees are 80% with average Y; what's the overall average?" Replace counts with proportions; the formula still works.

Proportion form If group i has weight wᵢ (with Σ wᵢ = 1), then
Overall Avg = Σ wᵢ × Aᵢ

4.4The CAT 2023 manufacturing-salary problem

CAT 2023 — Manufacturing salary ratio Hard

In a company, 20% of the employees work in manufacturing. The total salary paid to manufacturing employees is one-sixth of the total salary paid to all employees. What is the ratio of the average salary of manufacturing employees to the average salary of non-manufacturing employees?

  • 4 : 5
  • 5 : 6
  • 5 : 4
  • 6 : 5
STEP 1 · Translate to totals (always!) Let total employees = 100, total salary = S. Manufacturing employees = 20, salary paid to them = S/6. Non-manufacturing = 80, salary paid to them = S − S/6 = 5S/6.
STEP 2 · Compute the averages Avg salary (manuf) = (S/6)/20 = S/120.
Avg salary (non-manuf) = (5S/6)/80 = 5S/480 = S/96.
STEP 3 · Ratio (S/120) : (S/96) = 96 : 120 = 4 : 5.
AnswerOption A · 4 : 5

Teaching note: the entire question collapses once you assume total = 100 and total salary = S. Picking the right symbolic "anchor" is a skill — encourage students to write it down before computing.

4.5The merge-with-known-result problem

Reverse direction — find one group's average Medium

A class has 50 boys and 30 girls. The class average score is 64. The girls' average is 70. What is the boys' average?

STEP 1 · Total Total class score = 80 × 64 = 5120.
STEP 2 · Girls' contribution Girls' total = 30 × 70 = 2100. Boys' total = 5120 − 2100 = 3020. Boys' average = 3020/50 = 60.4.
AnswerBoys' average = 60.4

Module 05 · The Shortcut

The rule of
alligation.

A two-hundred-year-old shortcut from commercial arithmetic that turns most weighted-average CAT questions into ten-second one-liners. If your students learn nothing else this week, teach this.

5.1The rule in plain English

When two groups with averages A₁ and A₂ are merged to produce a combined average M, the ratio of their counts is the inverse of the ratio of their distances from the mean.

Alligation Rule n₁ : n₂ = (A₂ − M) : (M − A₁)
(where A₁ < M < A₂)

The group closer to the combined mean is the larger group.

5.2The visual — the alligation cross

The rule has a traditional diagrammatic form, sometimes called the alligation cross. It is faster than the formula once you internalise it:

The ratio n₁ : n₂ reads OFF THE BOTTOM corners.

Mechanics: write the two endpoints at the top. Write the mean in the middle. Compute each diagonal difference. The bottom row gives the ratio — and crucially, the bottom number on the left belongs to the left group, the bottom number on the right to the right group.

5.3Worked illustration

Alligation in action — milk mixture Easy

In what ratio must milk priced at Rs. 50/litre be mixed with milk priced at Rs. 70/litre to get a mixture worth Rs. 56/litre?

SET UP THE CROSS Left = 50, Right = 70, Middle = 56.
Bottom-left = 70 − 56 = 14. Bottom-right = 56 − 50 = 6.
So 50-rupee milk : 70-rupee milk = 14 : 6 = 7 : 3.
Answer7 : 3

Teaching note: pause and sanity-check the direction. The mixture (56) is closer to the cheaper milk (50), so the cheaper milk dominates the mix — hence the 7 : 3 ratio with cheaper milk on top. If your alligation answer doesn't pass this sanity check, you flipped the diagonal.

5.4Where alligation slays in CAT

Alligation is not just for mixtures (despite the name). Any time CAT asks you to combine two groups to hit a target average — combining classes, blending salary tiers, mixing investment returns — the rule gives you the ratio directly. Five seconds.

Alligation on salaries (non-mixture) Medium

A company has two teams. The average salary in Team A is Rs. 45,000 and in Team B is Rs. 65,000. The overall average across both teams is Rs. 53,000. What is the ratio of the number of employees in Team A to Team B?

ALLIGATION Left = 45, Right = 65, Middle = 53.
Bottom-left = 65 − 53 = 12. Bottom-right = 53 − 45 = 8.
Team A : Team B = 12 : 8 = 3 : 2.
Answer3 : 2

5.5Three-group alligation — when not to bother

For three or more groups, alligation no longer gives a unique answer (more unknowns than equations). In that case, fall back to the weighted-average formula and set up algebra. Don't try to apply the alligation cross to three groups; it will mislead you. Two-group only.

The "twin twins" trick. For three-group problems, sometimes you can pair two of the groups by their weighted average first, then alligate that pair against the third group. Useful in CAT problems that mix three salary tiers or three investment classes — see the CAT 2025 Kamala investment problem (from the percentages codex) for a related example.

Module 06 · Applications

Where averages
actually live
in CAT.

Eight applied themes — class scores, batting innings, salaries, age groups, speed/time, mixtures, business metrics, and the curious case of "average of averages".

6.1Theme — Class scores and grade averages

The bread and butter. A class has some students, an average mark, and either gains, loses, or has one student's score corrected. Always work in sums. Always.

6.2Theme — Batting averages (with not-outs)

The cricket twist: a batsman's average is total runs ÷ number of times dismissed, NOT total runs ÷ number of innings. Innings in which the batsman is "not out" count toward runs but not toward dismissals. This subtle redefinition often appears in CAT-style problems and almost always traps the casual reader.

Cricket Batting Average Batting Avg = Total Runs / Innings in which dismissed
Not-out innings add to numerator but not denominator.
Not-out cricket problem Medium

A batsman plays 12 innings, scoring a total of 540 runs, and remains not out in 2 of them. What is his batting average?

FORMULA 540 runs / (12 − 2) dismissals = 540/10 = 54.
AnswerBatting average = 54

6.3Theme — Salaries, bonuses, and corporate averages

CAT 2022's bonus problem (Q22 in the Problem Bank) is the gold standard. Overlapping subsets of employees, each with its own average, all linked together by sum conservation. The same technique you teach in Module 1 — work in sums, not averages — cracks it open.

6.4Theme — Ages and family problems

Classic family-age problem Medium

The average age of a family of 6 members today is 25 years. The youngest member is 10 years old. What was the average age of the family at the time the youngest member was born?

STEP 1 · Total today Total age today = 6 × 25 = 150 years.
STEP 2 · Roll back 10 years At the moment of the youngest's birth, the family had 5 members (not 6). Each of those 5 was 10 years younger. So total age then = 150 − 60 − 0 = 90 years (we subtract 10 × 5 = 50 for the others' ages, and the youngest's 10 — i.e. 60 in total).
Average age then = 90/5 = 18 years.
Answer18 years

Teaching note: the trap is forgetting that the youngest member didn't exist at that time. The count was 5, not 6. Students who try to subtract "everyone's 10 years" from the current total and divide by 6 get the wrong answer.

6.5Theme — Average speed

One of the most heavily tested traps. The average speed for a journey is not the average of the speeds. It is total distance divided by total time. When equal distances are travelled at different speeds, the average speed is the harmonic mean.

Average Speed Avg Speed = Total Distance / Total Time
For equal distances at speeds u and v: Avg Speed = 2uv/(u+v)

For instance, going 60 km at 30 km/h and returning at 60 km/h: the average speed is 2 × 30 × 60 / (30 + 60) = 3600/90 = 40 km/h. Not 45. The slower leg takes more time, dragging the average down.

6.6Theme — Mixtures (averages of concentrations)

Already met in Module 5 via alligation. Mixture problems are the most natural setting for the alligation cross because everything has units of "per litre" or "per kg" and the rule applies cleanly.

6.7Theme — Average of averages (when allowed?)

Average of averages is wrong unless weights are equal. If class A and class B have equal numbers of students, then the average of their averages equals the combined average. If the class sizes differ, you must use weighted averages. CAT setters routinely build options around this trap — option (D) is often the naive average of averages, sitting there to catch the rushing student.

6.8Theme — Median, mode, and other averages

Rarely tested directly in CAT. When they do appear, it's in DI sets or in the very occasional QA question. Definitions to know: median = middle value (or average of two middle values for even count); mode = most frequent value. For symmetric distributions all three coincide; for skewed data they pull apart, with the mean drifting toward the long tail.

Module 07 · Tactics

Twelve tips,
tricks, and traps.

Accumulated wisdom from teaching averages — what to drill, what to whisper, and the mistakes you will see again and again in classroom mocks.

TIP 01 · TOTALS
Always translate to sums first

The master identity Sum = Average × Count is the first thing on your scratch pad. Always. The student who reaches for it first solves twice as fast as one who carries averages around.

TIP 02 · DEVIATIONS
Sum of deviations is zero

When a problem gives partial info about how some values exceed or fall short of the mean, the rest must cancel them out exactly. This identity is gold for "find the missing total" problems.

TIP 03 · COUNT
Track count carefully

Did the youngest member exist back then? Was the coach already in the squad? Count is the most-missed variable. Always pause and ask: "how many are there now?"

TIP 04 · ALLIGATION
Use the cross for two groups

Whenever two groups merge to a known combined mean and you need the ratio, alligation gives it in five seconds. Don't reach for algebra unless three or more groups are involved.

TIP 05 · NOT-OUT
Cricket averages divide by dismissals

In any batting problem, the denominator is "innings in which dismissed", not "innings played". Not-out innings count toward runs but not toward the divisor.

TIP 06 · SPEED
Speed averaging is harmonic

For equal distances, use 2uv/(u+v). Never the simple average. The slower segment takes more time, so the average pulls toward the slower speed.

TIP 07 · WEIGHTS
Average of averages ≠ overall average

Unless the group sizes are equal. CAT setters love putting the naïve answer (simple average of averages) as a distractor. Always check the weights.

TIP 08 · APs
For arithmetic progressions, use endpoints

Mean of an AP equals (first + last)/2. Use this for any consecutive-integer, multiples-of-k, or evenly-spaced sequence. Don't sum term by term.

TIP 09 · BOUNDS
Mean lies between min and max

For "find the maximum possible average" problems, push the unconstrained values to their extreme. For "minimum possible", push them the other way. Always respect the global min/max bound.

TIP 10 · SHIFT & SCALE
Affine transforms transfer cleanly

If every value is multiplied by c and shifted by k, the mean does the same. Useful for "five years later" or "convert marks to percentages" problems.

TIP 11 · DROP
Removing an above-mean element drops the mean

And vice versa. Use this to predict the direction of change before computing. A sanity check saves you from sign errors.

TIP 12 · ALGEBRA
Two unknowns, two averages

If a problem gives you two distinct averages of two related groups (e.g., first n, first n+1), set up two equations on totals. The variable of interest is usually the (n+1)th element.

7.1Five traps that wreck the median student

Trap 1 — Naïve averaging of averages. Two classes have averages 60 and 80; the combined average is 70 only if class sizes are equal. Most CAT distractors are built on this.

Trap 2 — Forgetting the count changed. When a person joins or leaves, the count goes up or down. Half of students get the sum right and divide by the wrong number.

Trap 3 — Speed averaged arithmetically. "Bus goes at 40 km/h and returns at 60 km/h; average speed?" — almost everyone says 50. The correct answer is 48. This trap is so reliable it appears in CAT mocks every year.

Trap 4 — Counting not-out innings as dismissals. The batting-average denominator excludes not-outs. CAT mocks routinely embed this in mixed-section DI problems.

Trap 5 — Mishandling overlapping subsets. CAT 2022's bonus problem (Q22 ahead) gives averages of overlapping subsets. Adding the averages together overcounts. Track which employees belong to which subset.

7.2The two-minute problem checklist

  1. Read twice. Identify each group and its count.
  2. Translate every average to a sum. Write the sums down.
  3. Determine what's conserved (total sum? total count?) and what's changing.
  4. Set up one equation per piece of information.
  5. If two groups merge, consider alligation before algebra.
  6. Sanity-check direction: does the answer move the right way?
  7. Plug the answer back into the totals to verify.

Q21 · CAT 2022 — six distinct natural numbersElite

Consider six distinct natural numbers such that the average of the two smallest is 14, and the average of the two largest is 28. Find the maximum possible value of the average of all six numbers.

  • 22.5
  • 23.5
  • 24
  • 23
STEP 1 · The endpoint constraintsSum of two smallest = 28. Sum of two largest = 56.
STEP 2 · To maximise the overall average, maximise the two middle numbersThe two middle numbers must be distinct natural numbers, strictly greater than the second-smallest and strictly less than the second-largest.
The smallest pair sums to 28 with distinct positives — to leave room above, take them as (1, 27) — but wait: the second-smallest must be strictly less than both middle numbers. To maximise, we want the second-smallest as high as possible. Best case: smallest = 13, second-smallest = 15. (We can't take 14, 14 — must be distinct.)
The largest pair sums to 56 with distinct — say second-largest = 27, largest = 29. Then the two middle numbers must be in (15, 27): take them as 25, 26 to push the average up.
STEP 3 · ComputeSum = 13 + 15 + 25 + 26 + 27 + 29 = 135. Average = 135/6 = 22.5.
AnswerOption A · 22.5

Teaching note: this is the gold standard CAT averages problem — it tests structural reasoning (the bound property from Module 2) more than arithmetic. The temptation to grab any six numbers is the trap. Slow careful enumeration of the constraints wins.

Q22 · CAT 2022 — corporate bonus averagesElite

A company has 40 employees in a fixed order. In 2022, the average bonus of the first 30 employees was Rs. 40,000, of the last 30 employees was Rs. 60,000, and of the first 10 and last 10 together was Rs. 50,000. Next year, the average bonus of the first 10 employees rose by 100%, of the last 10 employees by 200%, and the rest were unchanged. What was the 2023 average bonus across all 40 employees?

  • 95000
  • 85000
  • 80000
  • 90000
STEP 1 · Label the groupsLet x = avg of first 10, y = avg of next 10 (positions 11–20), z = avg of next 10 (positions 21–30), w = avg of last 10 (positions 31–40).
STEP 2 · Use the three constraintsFirst 30 avg 40,000: 10x + 10y + 10z = 30 × 40,000 = 12,00,000 ⟹ x + y + z = 1,20,000.
Last 30 avg 60,000: 10y + 10z + 10w = 30 × 60,000 = 18,00,000 ⟹ y + z + w = 1,80,000.
First 10 and last 10 avg 50,000: 10x + 10w = 20 × 50,000 = 10,00,000 ⟹ x + w = 1,00,000.
STEP 3 · SolveSubtract the first from the second: w − x = 60,000. Combined with x + w = 1,00,000: w = 80,000, x = 20,000.
STEP 4 · 2023 averagesNew x = 2x = 40,000. New w = 3w = 2,40,000. y, z unchanged.
2023 total = 10 × 40,000 + 10y + 10z + 10 × 2,40,000 = 4,00,000 + 10(y + z) + 24,00,000.
From step 2: y + z = 1,20,000 − x = 1,00,000.
So total = 4,00,000 + 10,00,000 + 24,00,000 = 38,00,000.
Average = 38,00,000 / 40 = 95,000.
AnswerOption A · 95,000

Teaching note: this is a four-variable, three-equation system, and the variable of interest (the 2023 total) doesn't require solving for y and z individually — only for y + z. Teach the discipline of asking "what do I actually need?" before grinding through all four unknowns.

Q23 · CAT 2022 — non-decreasing sequence with replacementElite

The average of a non-decreasing sequence of N numbers a₁, a₂, …, aₙ is 300. If a₁ is replaced by 6a₁, the new average becomes 400. Find the number of possible values of a₁.

STEP 1 · TranslateOriginal sum = 300N. New sum = 400N. So 5a₁ = 100N ⟹ a₁ = 20N.
STEP 2 · Use the non-decreasing constraintSince the sequence is non-decreasing, a₁ ≤ each other term. Average is 300, so a₁ ≤ 300 ⟹ 20N ≤ 300 ⟹ N ≤ 15.
Also, N ≥ 2 (if N = 1, the average of a single number is just that number — and 6a₁ would give a "new average" of 6a₁; here we'd need 6a₁ = 400, but then original a₁ = 400/6 — and average of one number is itself, which was 300. Contradiction. So N ≥ 2.)
STEP 3 · CountN can take values 2, 3, …, 15. That's 14 values, each giving a distinct a₁.
Answer14
Q24 · Multi-stage average with constraintsElite

The average marks of 100 students were calculated as 40. Later, it was discovered that the marks of two students had been entered incorrectly as 75 and 81 instead of 57 and 18. After correction, what is the correct average?

METHODOriginal (incorrect) sum = 100 × 40 = 4000. Correction: subtract 75 and 81, add 57 and 18. Net change = (57 + 18) − (75 + 81) = 75 − 156 = −81. Corrected sum = 4000 − 81 = 3919. Corrected average = 3919/100 = 39.19.

A school has three classes A, B, and C with student-count ratio 4 : 5 : 6. The average score of class A is 60, of class B is 50, and the overall average across all three classes is 55. What is the average score of class C?

9.1Core definitions

The Arithmetic Mean Average = Sum / Count
Sum = Average × Count ← the workable form
Weighted Average (two groups) M = (n₁A₁ + n₂A₂) / (n₁ + n₂)
k-Group Weighted Average M = Σ(nᵢAᵢ) / Σnᵢ
Average Speed (round trip, equal distances) v_avg = 2uv / (u + v)

9.2Replacement, addition, removal — quick formulas

OperationChange in sumChange in mean
Replace v_out with v_inv_in − v_out(v_in − v_out) / n
Add v_new (count → n+1)v_new(v_new − M_old) / (n + 1)
Remove v_out (count → n−1)−v_out(M_old − v_out) / (n − 1)

9.3The five properties

  1. Shift. If every value gets +k, the mean gets +k.
  2. Scale. If every value gets ×c, the mean gets ×c.
  3. Bound. min ≤ Average ≤ max.
  4. Self-preserving. Adding a value equal to the mean keeps the mean.
  5. Symmetric set / AP. Mean = (first + last)/2 for any AP.

9.4Alligation cross (two groups only)

Endpoints A₁ and A₂ (A₁ < A₂); mean M; ratio of counts:

Alligation n₁ : n₂ = (A₂ − M) : (M − A₁)
The group closer to the mean is the bigger group.

9.5The traps catalogue

  • Average of averages ≠ overall average (unless weights equal).
  • Average speed is harmonic, not arithmetic.
  • Cricket: divide by dismissals, not innings.
  • When someone joins / leaves, the COUNT changes — track it.
  • The youngest didn't exist before they were born — careful with family-age problems.

9.6The two-minute problem checklist

  1. Read twice. Identify each group and its count.
  2. Translate every average to a sum. Write totals on scratch paper.
  3. Decide what's conserved and what's changing.
  4. If two groups merge, alligate. Otherwise, write equations on the totals.
  5. Sanity-check direction: does the answer move the right way?
  6. Plug back to verify.

9.7What to drill — recommended sequence

  1. Day 1: Foundations and the five properties — 45 minutes.
  2. Day 2: Replacement, addition, removal — 45 minutes plus drill set.
  3. Day 3: Weighted averages and alligation cross — 60 minutes.
  4. Day 4: Problem bank easy and medium — 90 minutes.
  5. Day 5: Hard and elite — at least three problems each.
  6. Weekly: revisit elite-tier problems and traps. Pair with percentages drills.
