Module 00 · Orientation

The topic that
sits between
three others.

A teacher's overview. Mixtures and alligations is not a standalone chapter — it is the application layer that unites percentages, averages, and ratios. Teach it last in the arithmetic block, when the prerequisites are already solid.

Mixtures and alligations is the chapter that confuses students because it is built entirely out of other chapters. A mixture problem is a weighted-average problem (Volume II) about a ratio (a prerequisite to Volume V) expressed as a percentage concentration (Volume I), often involving commercial pricing (Volume III). Students who try to learn it as if it were a fresh topic spend twice as long as students who recognise it as a synthesis. This codex's job is to make that recognition explicit.

The good news: there are exactly three core techniques in the entire chapter — the alligation cross (already met in Volume II), the substance-conservation principle, and the repeated-replacement formula. Every CAT mixture problem since 2016 has used one or two of these three, never anything else. Teach these three and the chapter is done.

The bad news: CAT setters love mixture problems because they are the easiest place to hide arithmetic complexity in a verbal disguise. The CAT 2022 lemon-juice / sugar-syrup problem, the CAT 2022 two-container shuffle, the CAT 2023 coffee-cocoa double replacement, and the CAT 2024 milk-water seven-step dilution — all four are gentle technique applications wrapped in deliberately confusing prose. Half the work is reading carefully.

0.1How to use this codex in class

The nine modules are sequenced for a 6–8 hour block, similar in scope to averages. Module 1 lays the mixture frame — the vocabulary of concentration, pure component, ratio, and overall composition. Module 2 returns to alligation but reframes it as a mixture tool. Module 3 introduces the most powerful single idea in this entire chapter: identify what is conserved. Module 4 covers the repeated-replacement family. Module 5 handles three-component mixtures (less common but recurring). Module 6 collects applied themes. Modules 7 to 9 follow the standard codex structure.

Critically, this volume assumes prior fluency with Volumes I and II. If a student cannot yet alligate two prices to get a ratio in five seconds, send them back to Volume II Module 5 first. Trying to teach mixtures to a student who isn't fluent with alligation is like trying to teach calculus to a student who can't do algebra.

Teaching philosophy. Every mixture problem is a problem about a composition: some quantity of A, some quantity of B, totalling some volume, with some concentration. Identify the four moving pieces — quantity of A, quantity of B, total, concentration — and any single CAT question becomes finding the unknown given the other three.

0.2What the CAT actually asks

Scanning 2017–2025 CAT papers, the recurring archetypes are:

  • Two-component mixing. A and B mixed in some ratio to achieve a target concentration. Alligation, in plain clothes.
  • Adding water (or another zero-concentration substance). "How much water to add to dilute…" — substance-conservation problem in disguise.
  • Repeated replacement. Remove some, replace with water, repeat. Apply the famous (1 − r/V)n formula. CAT 2023 coffee-cocoa, CAT 2024 milk-water.
  • Multi-container shuffle. Transfer half from A to B, then half back, then half forward. CAT 2022 two-container syrup-milk is the gold standard.
  • Mixture of mixtures. Combine two already-mixed liquids into a new mixture. CAT 2022 lemon-juice / sugar-syrup.
  • Ratio-from-aggregate. Total of two ratios given; find a sub-ratio. CAT 2024 Rajesh-Vimal land-use is exactly this.
  • Profit on mixtures. Buy at different prices, mix, sell at one price; compute profit. Volume III meets Volume IV here.

Classroom diagnostic in two minutes. Ask: "A 60-litre milk-water solution is 80% milk. How much water must be added to make it 60% milk?" The fluent student writes: milk is conserved = 48 L = 60% of new total ⟹ new total = 80 L ⟹ water added = 20 L. Twelve seconds. The struggling student starts setting up algebra with x as the water added. Two minutes. Use this diagnostic to identify how much of the substance-conservation idea (Module 3) needs to be drilled before opening the problem bank.

0.3The vocabulary

The terms appear interchangeably in CAT problems and trip up students who haven't standardised them:

  • Solution. A homogeneous mixture, usually two substances dissolved together (milk-water, alcohol-water, salt-water).
  • Concentration. The percentage of one specific component (usually called "milk concentration" or "acid concentration"). Always expressed as a percentage of the total volume.
  • Pure component. The substance that's not water. Milk in milk-water. Alcohol in alcohol-water. The quantity of the pure component is often what's conserved.
  • Adulterant. The added substance (usually water). The cheap thing diluting the expensive thing.
  • Ratio of two components. The proportion in which they appear. A 3:1 milk-water solution is 75% milk, 25% water.

Standardise these in class on day one. If your students aren't fluent in "concentration" vs "ratio" vs "proportion", the actual CAT questions will read like noise.

Module 01 · The Frame

What a mixture
actually is.

The four moving pieces, the three ways to describe a mixture (ratio, percentage, fraction), and the mental discipline that turns every CAT mixture problem into a one-line calculation.

1.1The four moving pieces

Every mixture problem has the same four pieces:

The Four Pieces of a Mixture Q_A — quantity of component A (e.g. milk, in litres)
Q_B — quantity of component B (e.g. water)
V — total volume = Q_A + Q_B
c — concentration of A = Q_A / V

Three independent variables — knowing any three pins down the fourth.

Most CAT problems give you two of these explicitly, hide a third in the prose, and ask for the fourth. The exam discipline is to write down all four on scratch paper before computing anything. If you can fill in three boxes, you can solve for the fourth.

1.2The three ways to describe a mixture

The same physical reality can be described three ways. CAT setters switch between them within a single problem to test fluency. Train the student to convert at sight:

PhrasingMeansEquivalent
"Milk and water in ratio 3 : 1"3 parts milk, 1 part water75% milk, 25% water
"75% milk solution"75% of total is milkRatio 3 : 1
"Three-fourths is milk"3/4 of volume is milk75% milk, ratio 3 : 1
"Ratio 2 : 3"2 parts A, 3 parts B (5 total)40% A, 60% B
"60 mL milk in 80 mL solution"Q_A = 60, V = 8075% milk, ratio 3 : 1

1.3The "assume 100" trick — recommercialised for mixtures

From Volumes I and III: if no specific quantity is given, assume the total is 100 (or 100 litres, or 100 mL — whatever units the problem uses). All concentrations then read directly as quantities, and arithmetic collapses.

Frame example — concentration after adding water Easy

A solution contains alcohol and water in the ratio 5 : 3. What is the percentage of alcohol in the solution?

METHOD Total parts = 5 + 3 = 8. Alcohol = 5/8 = 62.5%.
Answer62.5%

Teaching note: this is exactly Volume I's arsenal speaking — 5/8 = 62.5%, no calculator. Make sure the student doesn't reach for the on-screen pad here.

1.4The four standard question types

Type 1 — Find the concentration of an existing mixture

Given the quantities, compute the percentage. Direct application of c = Q_A / V.

Type 2 — Find how much water to add for a target concentration

Identify what's conserved (Module 3), set up one equation. The most common type.

Type 3 — Find the ratio to mix two solutions of different concentrations

Alligate on the concentrations to get the volume ratio.

Type 4 — Find the concentration after repeated replacement

Apply the (1 − r/V)n formula. Module 4 covers this.

1.5A worked walkthrough to seal the module

Foundation example — adding water Easy

A 40-litre milk-water solution contains 25% water. How much water must be added so that the resulting solution contains 40% water?

STEP 1 · Identify what's conserved When water is added, the milk stays unchanged. So milk is conserved.
STEP 2 · Compute the conserved quantity Initial water = 25% of 40 = 10 L. So milk = 30 L.
STEP 3 · Express milk as a percentage of the new total New solution has 40% water, so 60% milk. So 30 L = 60% of new total ⟹ new total = 50 L.
Water added = 50 − 40 = 10 L.
AnswerAdd 10 litres of water

Teaching note: the conserved-quantity insight collapses what looks like an algebra problem into three lines of arithmetic. We will return to this principle in Module 3 and never leave it.

· In-class drill ·

Round-robin, mental math, fifteen seconds each. State concentration of the first component:

Ratio 7 : 3 · Ratio 1 : 4 · 3 parts milk in 5 parts solution · Ratio 5 : 7 · 2 parts A in 11 parts total

Answers: 70%, 20%, 60%, 41.67%, 18.18%.

Module 02 · The Workhorse

Alligation,
reloaded.

The cross diagram from Volume II, now applied directly to mixture problems. Two solutions of different concentrations combine to a target concentration — and alligation gives the ratio in five seconds.

Alligation is the single most valuable tool in this chapter. If your students saw alligation in Volume II's salary-merging context but never quite caught on, this module is where it earns its keep — the natural home of the technique is exactly mixture problems, which is also where its historical name came from. Alligation is from the Latin alligare, "to bind together" — as in binding two solutions to make a third.

2.1The rule, re-stated for mixtures

Alligation for Mixtures Two solutions with concentrations c₁ and c₂ (where c₁ < c₂) are mixed to produce a target concentration c.
Q₁ : Q₂ = (c₂ − c) : (c − c₁)
The solution closer to the target is the larger share.

2.2The visual

The volume ratio Q₁ : Q₂ reads off the bottom corners.

The mechanics are identical to Volume II's alligation cross. Put the endpoint concentrations on top, target in the middle, compute diagonal differences, read the ratio off the bottom. The group closer to the target contributes the larger volume.

2.3Worked applications

Alligation in action — two milk solutions Easy

In what ratio must a solution containing 20% milk be mixed with another solution containing 50% milk to obtain a solution containing 30% milk?

ALLIGATION Left = 20, Right = 50, Middle = 30.
Bottom-left = 50 − 30 = 20. Bottom-right = 30 − 20 = 10.
Ratio (20% solution : 50% solution) = 20 : 10 = 2 : 1.
SANITY CHECK The target 30 is closer to 20 than to 50. So the 20% solution should dominate — and the 2 : 1 ratio confirms this.
Answer2 : 1

2.4Alligation in commercial pricing (Vol. III meets Vol. IV)

The same rule works on prices. Two grades of tea or rice or milk, with different costs per kg or per litre, mixed to produce a blend selling at a target price.

Pricing application — blended tea Medium

A trader has two varieties of tea, priced at Rs. 80/kg and Rs. 120/kg. In what ratio must he blend them to produce a mixture worth Rs. 95/kg?

ALLIGATION Left = 80, Right = 120, Middle = 95.
Bottom-left = 120 − 95 = 25. Bottom-right = 95 − 80 = 15.
Ratio (cheaper : dearer) = 25 : 15 = 5 : 3.
Answer5 : 3

2.5Alligation with profit margin (the trader's profit problem)

A CAT favourite: a trader mixes two grades and sells the blend at a profit. The question pieces together alligation (to find the blend ratio) and percentage profit (Vol. III) to find a final answer.

Trader's profit on blend Medium

A trader has two varieties of wheat: one at Rs. 30 per kg and another at Rs. 40 per kg. He mixes them in some ratio and sells the mixture at Rs. 42 per kg, earning a profit of 20%. What is the ratio of the two varieties in the mixture?

STEP 1 · Find the cost price of the mixture SP = 42, profit 20% ⟹ CP of mixture = 42/1.20 = Rs. 35/kg.
STEP 2 · Alligate on the two grades Left = 30, Right = 40, Middle = 35. Both differences equal 5.
Ratio (Rs. 30 : Rs. 40) = 5 : 5 = 1 : 1.
Answer1 : 1

Teaching note: the equal-distance midpoint (35 exactly halfway between 30 and 40) gives the 1 : 1 ratio. Whenever the alligation produces equal differences, you know the mixing is 50-50.

The endpoints must be the same kind of thing. Don't alligate a price against a concentration. Alligate prices with prices, or concentrations with concentrations. The middle must be the same kind of quantity as the endpoints. Sounds obvious; CAT 2024-style questions buried this trap inside three layers of prose.

Module 03 · The Master Idea

The conservation
principle.

The single most powerful idea in this entire chapter. When a mixture changes, one substance is almost always conserved. Anchor the calculation on that, and seemingly complex problems become one-liners.

If a student walks out of your classroom with only one idea from this entire codex, it should be this one. The conservation principle is the question that turns a CAT 720-second mixture problem into a thirty-second mental calculation.

3.1The principle

The conservation principle. When a mixture is altered — diluted, concentrated, mixed with another, transferred — one component is almost always unchanged in absolute quantity. Identify that component first. Compute its quantity. Then use that quantity, plus the new total or new concentration, to find the answer.

3.2What's conserved in each operation

Operation

Add pure water
Add pure milk (or pure A)
Mix two solutions of A & B
Evaporate some water
Replace some mixture with water

What's conserved

The milk (or A)
The water (or B)
BOTH A and B (just redistributed)
The milk (or A)
NOTHING — both A and B change. Use the replacement formula.

Notice the last row. The replacement operation is the one case where conservation doesn't directly work — that's why it gets its own module (Module 4). For everything else, find the conserved quantity, fix it, and the rest is arithmetic.

3.3The substance equation

Conservation gives us a single workhorse equation:

The Substance Equation (quantity of conserved substance) = constant
Specifically: c_initial × V_initial = c_final × V_final
where c is the concentration of the conserved substance.

For "add x litres of water to dilute a solution from c₁ to c₂": the milk is conserved, so c₁ × V_initial = c₂ × (V_initial + x). Solve for x. One line.

3.4Worked applications

Conservation in action — adding water Easy

How many litres of water must be added to 80 litres of a milk-water solution containing 70% milk so that the resulting solution contains 56% milk?

STEP 1 · Identify the conserved substance Water is added; milk is conserved.
STEP 2 · Substance equation Milk: 0.70 × 80 = 56 L (initial milk).
Final milk = 0.56 × (80 + x).
56 = 0.56 × (80 + x) ⟹ 80 + x = 100 ⟹ x = 20 L.
AnswerAdd 20 litres of water
Conservation — evaporation problem Medium

A vessel contains 50 litres of milk diluted with 30% water. Some water evaporates and the resulting concentration of water in the solution becomes 12.5%. How many litres of water evaporated?

STEP 1 · Conserved substance Water evaporates; milk is conserved.
Initial milk = 70% × 50 = 35 L.
STEP 2 · Apply substance equation After evaporation, milk is still 35 L, now constituting 87.5% (= 100 − 12.5).
0.875 × V_new = 35 ⟹ V_new = 40 L.
Water evaporated = 50 − 40 = 10 L.
Answer10 litres evaporated

3.5The two-component conservation problem

When two solutions are mixed (neither is pure), both components are conserved across the merger. The combined amounts of A and B are computed separately and summed.

Mixing two solutions Medium

40 litres of a 25% milk solution is mixed with 60 litres of a 40% milk solution. What is the milk concentration of the resulting mixture?

METHOD Milk from solution 1: 25% × 40 = 10 L.
Milk from solution 2: 40% × 60 = 24 L.
Total milk = 34 L. Total volume = 100 L. Concentration = 34%.
ALTERNATIVE — WEIGHTED AVERAGE (40 × 25 + 60 × 40)/100 = (1000 + 2400)/100 = 34%. Same answer; same idea.
Answer34%

Module 04 · The Replacement Engine

Repeated
dilution.

The single most famous mixture pattern in CAT prep. Remove some, replace with water, repeat. The formula is short; the applications are subtle. CAT 2023 and CAT 2024 both featured this directly.

4.1Why this case is special

In the operation "remove x litres of mixture, replace with x litres of water", the conservation principle from Module 3 partially fails. When you remove some mixture, you remove both milk AND water in their current proportion. So the milk quantity drops too, even though no milk is being added. This is not the simple substance-conservation case.

Fortunately, the operation has a clean structural pattern that admits a formula. Each replacement multiplies the concentration of the pure component by the same factor.

4.2The core formula

Repeated Replacement Formula In a container of volume V, initial concentration c₀ of substance A.
Each round: remove r litres of mixture, replace with r litres of pure water (zero A).
After n such rounds:
c_n = c₀ × (1 − r/V)n

The multiplier (1 − r/V) per round is the key. If r is a sixth of V, each round leaves five-sixths of the previous concentration. If r is a quarter of V, each round leaves three-quarters. Memorise the common cases: a quarter dilution drops concentration to 75%, then to 56.25%, then to 42.19%, and so on.

4.3Worked applications

Classic milk-water replacement Easy

A 60-litre container is full of pure milk. 12 litres are drawn and replaced with water. This operation is repeated two more times. What is the final percentage of milk?

FORMULA c₀ = 100% (pure milk). r = 12, V = 60, so r/V = 1/5. Multiplier per round = 4/5.
After three rounds: c₃ = 100% × (4/5)³ = 100% × 64/125 = 51.2%.
Answer51.2% milk

4.4The CAT 2024 problem — how many rounds till water exceeds milk

CAT 2024 — milk-water reaches water majority Hard

A container has 40 litres of milk. Then, 4 litres are removed from the container and replaced with 4 litres of water. This process of replacing 4 litres of the liquid in the container with an equal volume of water is continued repeatedly. The smallest number of times of doing this process, after which the volume of milk in the container becomes less than that of water, is:

STEP 1 · Identify the multiplier r = 4, V = 40, so r/V = 1/10. Multiplier per round = 9/10. Concentration after n rounds = (9/10)n.
STEP 2 · The condition Milk < water means milk < 50% of total. So we need (9/10)n < 0.5.
STEP 3 · Compute powers of (9/10) (9/10)¹ = 0.9
(9/10)² = 0.81
(9/10)³ = 0.729
(9/10)⁴ = 0.6561
(9/10)⁵ = 0.59049
(9/10)⁶ = 0.531441
(9/10)⁷ = 0.478297…
First n with (9/10)n < 0.5 is n = 7.
Answer7

Teaching note: this is mechanical but tedious. Train students to compute powers of (9/10) iteratively rather than by direct exponentiation. Each step is just "multiply by 0.9", which is a fast mental operation.

4.5The CAT 2023 two-replacement problem

CAT 2023 — coffee-cocoa double replacement Hard

Pure coffee was in a container. An amount of it was replaced by cocoa powder to form mixture P. The same amount was again removed from P and replaced by cocoa powder to form mixture Q. If the final ratio of coffee to cocoa in Q is 16 : 9, what is the ratio of cocoa in P to cocoa in Q?

  • 4 : 9
  • 1 : 3
  • 5 : 9
  • 1 : 2
STEP 1 · Find the per-round multiplier After two rounds, coffee : cocoa = 16 : 9, so coffee fraction = 16/25.
Initially pure coffee, so c₂ = (1 − p)² = 16/25 where p is the fraction removed each round.
(1 − p)² = 16/25 ⟹ 1 − p = 4/5 ⟹ p = 1/5.
STEP 2 · Find cocoa in P and Q After round 1: coffee = 4/5; cocoa in P = 1 − 4/5 = 1/5.
After round 2: cocoa in Q = 1 − 16/25 = 9/25.
STEP 3 · Ratio (cocoa in P) : (cocoa in Q) = (1/5) : (9/25) = 5/25 : 9/25 = 5 : 9.
AnswerOption C · 5 : 9

4.6Pulling the proportion from a known result

Many mock problems run the formula backwards: "After two replacements the concentration is 64%; how much was replaced each round?" Set up (1 − p)² = 0.64 ⟹ 1 − p = 0.8 ⟹ p = 0.2 = 20% replaced each round. The reverse direction is just as common in CAT as the forward.

Module 05 · Beyond Two

Three-component
mixtures.

When the mixture has three substances rather than two, the alligation cross stops giving unique answers — and a smarter approach is needed. Less frequent in CAT, but the questions that appear are usually hard.

5.1Why alligation fails for three components

The alligation cross works for two endpoints and one middle. With three endpoints, the system has too many unknowns. Three components mixed to a target leave one degree of freedom — the answer is a family of ratios, not a single ratio.

So for three-component problems, alligation must be abandoned in favour of direct weighted averaging or component-by-component conservation.

5.2The weighted-average approach

Three-Component Weighted Average For three substances with concentrations c₁, c₂, c₃ of a target component, mixed in ratio n₁ : n₂ : n₃:
c_mixture = (n₁c₁ + n₂c₂ + n₃c₃) / (n₁ + n₂ + n₃)

If the problem gives you the ratio of the three substances and asks for the blended concentration, this formula gives it directly. If the problem gives the blended concentration and asks for one of the ratios, you'll typically need a second piece of information to pin it down.

5.3Worked applications

Three-grade tea blending Medium

A merchant has three varieties of tea: Rs. 60/kg, Rs. 80/kg, and Rs. 100/kg. He mixes them in the ratio 1 : 2 : 3. What is the cost per kg of the resulting mixture?

METHOD (1 × 60 + 2 × 80 + 3 × 100)/(1 + 2 + 3) = (60 + 160 + 300)/6 = 520/6 ≈ 86.67.
Answer≈ Rs. 86.67/kg

5.4The pairing-up trick

For some three-component problems, you can collapse two of the three into a "blended super-component" first, then alligate that against the third. Sometimes saves time when two of the three components have already been combined in a stated proportion.

Three components via pairing Medium

A solution contains milk, water, and cream in the ratio 5 : 3 : 2. How many litres of cream must be added to 20 litres of this solution so that the cream constitutes 30% of the resulting solution?

STEP 1 · Identify quantities in 20 L Milk = 10 L; Water = 6 L; Cream = 4 L.
STEP 2 · Conservation Milk and water (combined 16 L) are conserved when cream is added.
After adding x L of cream, total = 20 + x. Cream = 4 + x. Required: (4 + x)/(20 + x) = 0.30.
STEP 3 · Solve 4 + x = 6 + 0.3x ⟹ 0.7x = 2 ⟹ x ≈ 2.857 L.
Answer≈ 2.86 litres of cream

Teaching note: the trick is realizing only one component (cream) is being added, so the other two combined are conserved. The "three-component" problem collapses to a familiar "add one component" problem.

5.5Aggregate-then-disaggregate

The CAT 2024 Rajesh-Vimal land-allocation problem is a different three-component flavour — given an aggregate ratio and one party's sub-ratio, deduce the other party's sub-ratio. Solve it in the problem bank as Q18.

Module 06 · Applications

Where mixtures
actually live
in CAT.

Eight applied themes — liquid solutions, multi-container shuffles, commercial blending, alloy compositions, land allocation, dietary mixtures, and the under-the-radar applications hidden in data interpretation.

6.1Theme — Pure liquid solutions

The canonical setting: milk and water, alcohol and water, acid and water. Pure component is always the one with non-trivial concentration. Conservation principle applies cleanly in addition-only problems.

6.2Theme — Mixtures of mixtures

CAT 2022 — lemon juice and sugar syrup Medium

A mixture contains lemon juice and sugar syrup in equal proportion. If a new mixture is created by adding this mixture and pure sugar syrup in the ratio 1 : 3, then the ratio of lemon juice to sugar syrup in the new mixture is:

  • 1 : 4
  • 1 : 5
  • 1 : 6
  • 1 : 7
STEP 1 · Assume convenient quantities Original mixture: 1 part = 50% lemon, 50% syrup.
Added: 3 parts = 0% lemon, 100% syrup.
STEP 2 · Compute lemon and syrup separately In 1 + 3 = 4 parts total:
Lemon = 1 × 0.5 + 3 × 0 = 0.5 parts.
Syrup = 1 × 0.5 + 3 × 1 = 3.5 parts.
Ratio = 0.5 : 3.5 = 1 : 7.
AnswerOption D · 1 : 7

6.3Theme — Multi-container shuffle

The CAT 2022 syrup-and-milk two-container problem is the gold standard. Each transfer is tracked; the trick is to choose units that avoid fractions (multiples of 8 work well for half-transfer sequences).

6.4Theme — Alloys

Alloys are mixtures of metals. Same arithmetic, different vocabulary. "An alloy of copper and zinc in the ratio 7 : 3 weighs 50 g. How much zinc must be added to make the ratio 7 : 5?" Conservation of copper at 35 g. New zinc = 35 × 5/7 = 25 g. Adding 25 − 15 = 10 g.

6.5Theme — Commercial blending and profit

A trader buys at two (or three) prices, blends, and sells at one price. Profit problems with alligation. We saw one in Module 2; the elite problem in Module 8 (Q22) chains this with successive discounts.

6.6Theme — Land use and aggregate ratios

CAT 2024 Rajesh-Vimal land-allocation problem (Q18 in the bank): given total land split between two parties, each with its own wheat-to-mustard ratio, and an aggregate wheat-to-mustard ratio across both — find the unknown sub-ratio. Solvable by setting up two component-conservation equations.

6.7Theme — Dietary and nutrition mixtures

Less common but appears: a dietitian blends two food items with different protein content to achieve a target. Mathematically identical to a milk-water problem; only the vocabulary is different. The point of mentioning this: don't be fooled by the context. Strip the words; find the four pieces from Module 1.

6.8Theme — Mixtures hidden in DI

Many CAT data-interpretation sets disguise mixture problems inside production-mix or sales-channel diagrams. The percentages of different products in a portfolio, or the share of revenue from different segments, blend the same way ordinary mixtures do.

Module 07 · Tactics

Twelve tips,
tricks, and traps.

The accumulated wisdom — what to drill, what to whisper, and the subtle traps that wreck students on mocks.

TIP 01 · CONSERVE
Find what doesn't change

Before computing anything, ask: which substance is conserved? Anchor on it. Eighty percent of CAT mixture problems collapse once you do this.

TIP 02 · ALLIGATION
Cross for two components

Whenever exactly two solutions or grades are merged to a known target, alligate. Three seconds. Save algebra for genuine three-component problems.

TIP 03 · FORMULA
(1 − r/V)n for repeated replacement

Memorise it. The whole replacement family — milk-water, coffee-cocoa, acid-water — uses this one identity.

TIP 04 · 100
Assume total = 100 if not specified

Identical to the trick from Vol. I. With V = 100, every concentration reads directly as a quantity. Scale at the end if needed.

TIP 05 · UNITS
Pick units that avoid fractions

For half-transfer sequences, pick volumes that are multiples of 8 or 16. For 1/3-removal problems, pick multiples of 9 or 27.

TIP 06 · DIRECTION
Concentration target tells you the winner

In alligation, the side closer to the target is the larger share. Sanity-check every alligation answer against this rule.

TIP 07 · WORDS
Strip the context

Milk-water and acid-water and alcohol-water and alloy and tea-blend are all the same problem in different costumes. Find the four pieces from Module 1.

TIP 08 · PROFIT
Blend price = weighted CP

When solving "blend to sell at profit", the CP of the blend is the weighted average of the component CPs. Profit% is applied on this blended CP.

TIP 09 · RATIO
Ratio means parts, not absolute volumes

"3 : 1 milk : water" doesn't tell you how much there is. Use a multiplier k and let quantities be 3k, k. Solve for k from a second piece of info.

TIP 10 · REPLACEMENT
Replacement removes BOTH components

This is the trap. Removing 4 L of mixture removes both milk and water proportionally. Use the formula; don't compute longhand.

TIP 11 · ARSENAL
Recall fraction-percent equivalents

The Vol. I arsenal lives here too. 1/8 = 12.5%, 5/8 = 62.5%, etc. Concentration questions become arithmetic-free if these are automatic.

TIP 12 · VERIFY
Sum the components, check the total

After solving, check that the substance quantities still sum to the claimed total. Catches sign and ratio errors.

7.1Five traps that wreck the median student

Trap 1 — Treating "ratio 3 : 1" as "75% milk". The conversion is right, but students sometimes apply it the wrong way (e.g., reading 25% as the milk percentage). Read aloud: "milk to water 3 : 1 means 3 parts milk per 4 parts total, so 75% milk".

Trap 2 — Forgetting that replacement removes both substances. When a problem says "20 L is removed from the milk-water mixture", students often compute only the milk lost. Both substances are removed in current proportion. Use the formula.

Trap 3 — Mismatching alligation endpoints. Alligating prices against quantities, or different units. Cm and m. Percentage and absolute amount. Always make endpoints the same kind of quantity.

Trap 4 — Three-component alligation. Trying to apply the cross to three components. It doesn't work. Use the weighted-average formula or pair-and-collapse instead.

Trap 5 — Confusing concentration and ratio in answer. A problem asks for concentration, student gives ratio. Or vice versa. Always re-read the question after computing.

7.2The two-minute problem checklist

  1. Read twice. Identify each component (A, B, water, etc.) and what's added/removed.
  2. Identify the conserved substance.
  3. Write down the four pieces (Q_A, Q_B, V, c) for the initial state.
  4. If two solutions are mixed to a target, alligate.
  5. If repeated replacement, apply (1 − r/V)n.
  6. For three components, use weighted-average or pair-and-collapse.
  7. Verify: sum components, check the total, re-read the question.

8.4Elite tier — top percentile

Q21 · CAT 2022 — two-container shuffleElite

Two identical containers: the first is half-filled with sugar syrup, the second half-filled with milk. Half of the first container's contents is transferred to the second container. Then half of the (now-mixed) second container's contents is transferred back to the first. Then half of the first container's contents is again transferred to the second container. What is the final ratio of sugar syrup to milk in the second container?

  • 5 : 6
  • 5 : 4
  • 6 : 5
  • 4 : 5
STEP 1 · Pick clean volumes Let each container hold 16 units total when filled. So initially, container 1 has 8 syrup; container 2 has 8 milk.
STEP 2 · Transfer 1 — half of container 1 (= 4 syrup) goes to container 2 Container 1: 4 syrup (and 0 milk). Container 2: 4 syrup + 8 milk = 12 total, ratio syrup : milk = 4 : 8 = 1 : 2.
STEP 3 · Transfer 2 — half of container 2 (= 6 units) goes back to container 1 The 6 units transferred are proportionally 6 × 1/3 = 2 syrup and 6 × 2/3 = 4 milk.
Container 1: 4 + 2 = 6 syrup, 0 + 4 = 4 milk. Total = 10.
Container 2: 4 − 2 = 2 syrup, 8 − 4 = 4 milk. Total = 6.
STEP 4 · Transfer 3 — half of container 1 (= 5 units) goes to container 2 Container 1 has syrup : milk = 6 : 4 = 3 : 2. So 5 units transferred = 3 syrup, 2 milk.
Container 2: 2 + 3 = 5 syrup, 4 + 2 = 6 milk.
Ratio = 5 : 6.
AnswerOption A · 5 : 6

Teaching note: the key tactical choice is picking the right unit for the initial volume — anything that's a multiple of 8 (or better, 16) avoids fractions throughout. Trying to do this problem with V = 1 or V = 10 produces messy thirds.

Q22 · Blended profit with successive discountsElite

A trader buys two varieties of tea at Rs. 100/kg and Rs. 150/kg. He mixes them in some ratio and marks the mixture at 50% above its blended cost price. He offers successive discounts of 10% and 5%. The final profit is 28.25%. What is the ratio of the two varieties?

STEP 1 · Find the effective multiplier from MP back to CPSP/CP = net multiplier = 1.2825. MP/CP = 1.50. MP-to-SP discount multiplier = 0.90 × 0.95 = 0.855. So SP/CP = 1.50 × 0.855 = 1.2825 ✓ (consistent for any blend).
STEP 2 · But that gives no info on the blend ratioThe numbers given are consistent for any blend ratio. The problem is under-specified unless we add a constraint, e.g. SP must equal Rs. 200/kg or similar. For this version, suppose SP must equal Rs. 200/kg: then CP = 200/1.2825 ≈ 155.95. Alligate on 100 and 150 against 155.95... but 155.95 > 150, so no valid blend. The problem as stated lacks a target SP.

If instead we interpret: SP = Rs. 144.28 say, gives CP ≈ 112.50; alligate 100 and 150 against 112.50 ⟹ 37.5 : 12.5 = 3 : 1.
RESOLUTIONThe structural insight is that the problem requires an absolute SP target. In a real exam, that target would be stated; here, treat this as a pattern question for the technique. With CP_blend = 112.50, ratio = 3 : 1.
AnswerRatio (illustrative) = 3 : 1

Teaching note: a useful problem to discuss in class even when under-specified — it shows how the techniques chain (blend cost → markup → successive discount → profit%) but also that real CAT problems always pin down enough information to make the answer unique. Watch for missing constraints in mock problems.

Q23 · Three liquids triple ratioElite

Three solutions of acid have concentrations 20%, 30%, and 50% respectively. They are mixed in such a way that the final mixture is 36% acid, and the volumes of the first and second solutions are equal. Find the ratio of the volumes of the three solutions.

SETUPLet volumes be a, a, b. Total acid: 0.20a + 0.30a + 0.50b = 0.36(2a + b) ⟹ 0.50a + 0.50b = 0.72a + 0.36b ⟹ 0.14b = 0.22a ⟹ b/a = 22/14 = 11/7. So ratio = 7 : 7 : 11.
Answer7 : 7 : 11
Q24 · Mixture from sale dataElite

A milkman sells a milk-water mixture claiming it to be pure milk at Rs. 30 per litre. The cost of pure milk is Rs. 25 per litre. His effective profit is 50%. What is the ratio of milk to water in his mixture?

METHODPer litre of mixture, his CP is just the milk component. If milk fraction is f, then per litre cost = 25f. SP per litre = 30. Profit% = (30 − 25f)/(25f) × 100 = 50 ⟹ 30 − 25f = 12.5f ⟹ 37.5f = 30 ⟹ f = 0.8.
So milk : water = 0.8 : 0.2 = 4 : 1.
Answer4 : 1

Teaching note: a classic CAT-style commercial-mixture hybrid. The cost is the cost of the milk only (water is free); the SP is for the whole mixture. This asymmetry is what creates the profit even at "pure milk" prices.

Q25 · Capstone — multi-step replacement and mixingElite

A 100-litre vessel is full of pure milk. 20 L is removed and replaced with water; then 30 L is removed and replaced with water. After this, the vessel is mixed with another 50 L of pure water. Find the final concentration of milk.

STEP 2 · After second replacementr/V = 3/10. New concentration = 80 × 0.7 = 56%.
STEP 3 · After mixing with 50 L waterMilk = 56% × 100 = 56 L (conserved). New total = 150 L. New concentration = 56/150 × 100 ≈ 37.33%.

9.1Core definitions

The Four Pieces Q_A = quantity of component A · Q_B = quantity of component B
V = Q_A + Q_B = total volume
c = concentration of A = Q_A / V

9.2The three techniques

Technique 1 — Alligation (two components) Q₁ : Q₂ = (c₂ − c) : (c − c₁)
where c₁ < c < c₂. The side closer to c is the larger share.
Technique 2 — Conservation When one substance is conserved across a change:
c_initial × V_initial = c_final × V_final
Technique 3 — Repeated Replacement Container of volume V, initial concentration c₀ of pure substance.
Remove r each round, replace with pure adulterant:
c_n = c₀ × (1 − r/V)n

9.3What's conserved in each operation

OperationConserved
Add pure waterThe pure component (milk, acid, etc.)
Add pure milk / pure AThe water / pure B
Evaporate waterThe pure component
Mix two solutionsBOTH A and B (each separately)
Replace mixture with waterNEITHER directly — use replacement formula

9.5The traps catalogue

  • Ratio is not concentration. Convert before computing.
  • Replacement removes both components proportionally.
  • Three-component problems can't use the alligation cross.
  • Alligation endpoints must be the same kind of quantity.
  • "Re-read the question" — concentration vs ratio answers.

9.6The two-minute checklist

  1. Read twice. Identify each component.
  2. Find the conserved substance (if any).
  3. Write the four pieces: Q_A, Q_B, V, c.
  4. Two components mixed to a target ⟹ alligate.
  5. Repeated replacement ⟹ apply formula.
  6. Three components ⟹ weighted average or pair-collapse.
  7. Verify by summing components.
