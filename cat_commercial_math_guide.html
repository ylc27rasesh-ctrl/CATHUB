Module 00 · Orientation

Why this is
one topic,
not three.

A teacher's overview. Profit-and-loss, discount, simple interest, and compound interest are not separate chapters. They are one chapter — commercial math — told from four different vantage points. Teach them together and the entire block makes sense.

Most CAT prep books treat profit-and-loss in chapter four, simple interest in chapter seven, compound interest in chapter eight, and discount somewhere in the middle. This is a teaching disaster. The four topics share one underlying idea — money changes hands, and we account for the difference as a percentage of some reference. Once a student sees this, the four topics dissolve into one set of techniques applied to four contexts. Two weeks of confused study become two days of clarity.

This is also why the percentages codex (Volume I) is the prerequisite for everything in this volume. If your student is still struggling with "20% off then 10% extra discount" as a successive-change problem, send them back to Volume I before opening this one. The arithmetic here is not new; the application is.

0.1How to use this codex in class

The nine modules are sequenced for an 8–10 hour block of teaching, structured as one continuous narrative rather than four mini-chapters. Module 1 lays the unified commercial-math frame — the same Cost Price–Selling Price–Marked Price–Discount vocabulary will reappear, dressed slightly differently, in interest problems as Principal–Amount–Rate–Time. Modules 2 and 3 are the profit-loss-discount block, modules 4 and 5 the interest block, module 6 the hybrids, and the remainder follows the standard codex structure.

The recent CAT trend (especially 2023 and 2024) is toward multi-stage commercial problems — Minu sells to Kanu who sells back to Minu who sells to Tanu; Bina sells to Shyam who sells to Hari; Anil borrows at compound interest, repays partially, then pays off the rest. These are CAT setters explicitly testing the unified frame. If your student thinks of profit-loss and interest as separate topics, they will lose these questions in the first ten seconds.

The unifying claim. Every problem in this volume is a problem about a quantity that changes from a reference value. Profit/loss is a change from Cost Price. Discount is a change from Marked Price. Interest is a change in Principal. The mechanics — multipliers, percentages, ratios — are identical across all four. Only the labels change.

0.2What the CAT actually asks

From the 2017–2025 CAT papers, the recurring archetypes are:

  • Direct profit/loss percentage problems. Two prices given; compute the third quantity (often as a percentage). Gentle warm-ups.
  • Markup-then-discount chains. Mark up by X%, discount by Y%, what's the net profit? Successive-percentage thinking from Volume I, dressed in commercial vocabulary.
  • Two-object pricing. One item profits by X%, the other loses by Y%, both sold at the same price. Find net effect. CAT 2023 Gita's two objects is the gold-standard problem here.
  • Multi-party transactions. A sells to B who sells to C; the chain accumulates profits and losses. Recent CAT favourite.
  • False weights / dishonest dealer. "Charges for 1 kg but gives 950 g." A subtle effective-rate calculation.
  • Cost-restructuring problems. "If cost were 40% lower, profit would be…" — CAT 2024 archetype.
  • Simple interest splits. Capital divided across rates; find the unknown rate. CAT 2022 Nitu's banks is the classic.
  • Compound interest with partial payments. Loan with intermediate repayments — CAT 2023 Anil's loan.
  • SI–CI hybrids. Money in CI scheme, then moved to SI scheme — CAT 2023 Anil–Sunil.
  • SI–CI difference problems. "Difference between CI and SI for two years at r% is Rs. X; find the principal." A formula-driven micro-genre.

Classroom diagnostic in two minutes. Ask: "A shopkeeper marks up by 25% and then offers a 20% discount. Is there profit or loss, and how much?" Strong students answer instantly: 1.25 × 0.80 = 1.00, so neither profit nor loss. Median students compute the markup and discount separately and get tangled. Weak students answer "5% loss" by subtracting the percentages. Use this diagnostic to identify how much of Volume I needs to be re-anchored before pushing into the question bank.

0.3The vocabulary problem

The single greatest barrier to fluency in this topic is the volume of vocabulary. Cost price, selling price, marked price, list price, discount, markup, profit, loss, principal, amount, interest, rate, time, compounded annually, compounded half-yearly. Eight terms in P&L; six in interest; and CAT setters routinely use the technical names without defining them. A student who cannot rapidly translate "marked price" to MP and "selling price" to SP is already behind. Module 1 gives the full glossary and the standard symbols. Memorise them.

Module 01 · The Frame

The commercial
math frame:
one vocabulary,
one engine.

The glossary, the symbols, and the single mental model that turns four chapters into one. By the end of this module, students should be fluent in moving between CP, SP, MP, P, MP × (1 − d) — and seeing the parallel with Principal, Rate, Time.

1.1The four terms of P&L — defined, distinguished, drilled

The Profit-Loss Vocabulary CP · Cost Price: what the seller paid
SP · Selling Price: what the seller charged the buyer
MP · Marked Price: the sticker / list price before discount
Discount: the reduction from MP to SP
Markup: the increase from CP to MP
Profit: SP − CP (when positive)
Loss: CP − SP (when positive)

Notice the four quantities are linked by a chain:

This single diagram is the entire profit-loss-discount topic. Read it left-to-right: the seller starts with cost (CP), marks it up to MP, may offer a discount, lands at SP. The difference between SP and CP is the profit or loss. Markup is on CP. Discount is on MP. These are NOT the same base. Half the trap problems in CAT exploit this distinction.

1.2The four key formulas of P&L

The Four Core Formulas MP = CP × (1 + markup%/100)
SP = MP × (1 − discount%/100)
Profit% = (SP − CP)/CP × 100
Loss% = (CP − SP)/CP × 100

Note: profit and loss are always computed against cost price, never against selling price. Discount is computed against marked price. Markup is computed against cost price. The three percentages have three different bases. The student who internalises this is two-thirds of the way through this entire volume.

1.3The interest vocabulary — and its parallel

The Interest Vocabulary P · Principal: the original sum
R · Rate of interest, % per period
T · Time, in number of periods
SI · Simple Interest (the linear engine)
CI · Compound Interest (the exponential engine)
A · Amount: Principal + accumulated Interest

Look at the parallel:

Profit-loss

CP is the reference. The transaction is a one-step change. SP is the final value. The change is expressed as profit% on CP.

Interest

P is the reference. The transaction is a multi-period change. A is the final value. The change is expressed as rate% per period.

Same idea, slightly different temporal scaling. Profit-loss is interest with the time component collapsed to one transaction. Compound interest is profit-loss with the markup applied repeatedly. Once a student sees this — and Volume I has taught them successive percentage change — they don't need to learn compound interest as a new topic. They already know it.

1.4The master identity for the entire volume

The unifying identity. Every commercial-math problem can be rewritten as: Final = Initial × (multiplier). The multiplier is (1 + p%/100) for a profit, (1 − d%/100) for a discount, (1 + r/100) per period for compound interest, and the more elaborate (1 + RT/100) for the entire simple-interest stretch. Once a student writes down the multiplier, the rest of the problem is arithmetic.

1.5A worked walkthrough to seal the module

Foundation example — markup, discount, and net profit Easy

A shopkeeper buys an article for Rs. 800. He marks it up by 50% and then gives the customer a 20% discount. What is his profit percentage?

STEP 1 · Build the multipliers Markup multiplier on CP: 1.50.
Discount multiplier on MP: 0.80.
Net multiplier on CP: 1.50 × 0.80 = 1.20.
STEP 2 · Translate to percentage SP = 1.20 × CP, so profit% = 20%.
STEP 3 · Sanity-check with rupee figures MP = 800 × 1.50 = 1200. SP = 1200 × 0.80 = 960. Profit = 960 − 800 = 160. Profit% = 160/800 = 20%. ✓
AnswerProfit = 20%

Teaching note: the multiplier method handles markup–discount problems in three seconds. Without it, a student writes three equations. Drill this until automatic.

· In-class drill ·

Round-robin, mental math, ten seconds each. Compute the net effect (profit% or loss%):

+25% markup, 20% discount · +40% markup, 25% discount · +33% markup, 25% discount · +20% markup, 25% discount

Answers: 0% (1.25 × 0.80 = 1.00). +5% (1.40 × 0.75 = 1.05). −0.25% (≈ break-even). −10% (1.20 × 0.75 = 0.90).

Module 02 · Engines I

The markup–
discount chain.

The first engine. Markup, discount, profit, loss, all as one chain — solved with the multiplier method from Volume I. No new formulas; only new applications.

2.1The multiplier method, recommercialised

From Volume I: every percentage change is a multiplier. Marked up by 25%? Multiply by 1.25. Discounted by 20%? Multiply by 0.80. Apply both? Multiply the multipliers. This is the entire chapter on profit-loss-discount, expressed in eight words.

The Master Multiplier for P&L SP / CP = (1 + markup) × (1 − discount)

The right-hand side is the net multiplier. If > 1, profit. If < 1, loss. If = 1, break-even.

2.2The four standard question types

Type 1 — Given markup and discount, find profit%

Compute the net multiplier directly. Marked up by 30%, discount of 10%: net = 1.30 × 0.90 = 1.17, profit = 17%. Five seconds, no algebra.

Type 2 — Given net profit% and one of markup/discount, find the other

Equate the multipliers. If 20% profit is desired after a 25% discount: 1 + markup = 1.20 / 0.75 = 1.60, so markup = 60%.

Type 3 — Find the SP, given everything but the rupee figure

Apply the net multiplier to the CP. If CP = 500, markup 40%, discount 10%: SP = 500 × 1.40 × 0.90 = 630.

Type 4 — Reverse-engineer the CP from a stated profit

Divide the SP by the net multiplier. If SP = 920, profit 15%: CP = 920 / 1.15 = 800.

2.3Successive discounts

A favourite CAT trick: two discounts in succession (often expressed as "20% off, plus another 15% off at checkout"). The two discounts multiply, never add.

Successive Discounts Net discount multiplier = (1 − d₁/100) × (1 − d₂/100)
For 20% and 15%: 0.80 × 0.85 = 0.68 → net 32% off.

The trap. 20% off and 15% off does NOT equal 35% off. The second discount applies to the price after the first discount, which is already smaller. The net is 32%, not 35%. CAT setters embed this in option (D) every chance they get.

2.4Worked applications

CAT 2024 — Gopi marks for 20% profit, Ravi gets 10% discount Medium

Gopi marks a price on a product in order to make 20% profit. Ravi gets a 10% discount on this marked price, and thus saves Rs. 15. The profit, in rupees, made by Gopi by selling the product to Ravi, is:

  • 10
  • 20
  • 15
  • 25
STEP 1 · Set up the chain Let CP = x. Then MP = 1.20x. Ravi's SP after 10% discount = 0.90 × 1.20x = 1.08x.
Ravi's saving = MP − SP = 1.20x − 1.08x = 0.12x = 15.
So x = 125.
STEP 2 · Compute Gopi's profit Profit = SP − CP = 1.08x − x = 0.08x = 0.08 × 125 = 10.
AnswerOption A · Rs. 10

Teaching note: the question is verbal but the underlying structure is one chain — CP → MP → SP. Drilled chains take ninety seconds; algebraic guesswork takes five minutes.

2.5The "find profit if cost-restructured" problem

CAT 2024 — selling price unchanged, cost reduced Medium

The selling price of a product is fixed to ensure 40% profit. If the product had cost 40% less and had been sold for 5 rupees less, then the resulting profit would have been 50%. The original selling price, in rupees, of the product is:

  • 10
  • 14
  • 15
  • 20
STEP 1 · Original Let CP = C. SP = 1.40 C.
STEP 2 · Restructured New CP = 0.6 C. New SP = 1.40 C − 5. New profit% = 50%, so new SP = 1.5 × 0.6 C = 0.9 C.
STEP 3 · Solve 1.40 C − 5 = 0.9 C ⟹ 0.5 C = 5 ⟹ C = 10. Original SP = 1.40 × 10 = 14.
AnswerOption B · Rs. 14

Module 03 · Engines II

False weights
and the two-object
parity problem.

Two specialised P&L sub-genres that CAT setters love. Each has a one-line trick once understood — and each humiliates students who try to solve it longhand.

3.1The two-object problem — the parity trap

The classic setup: a seller sells two articles at the same SP. On one she gains x%, on the other she loses x%. What's the overall effect? The naive answer — break-even — is wrong. There is always a net loss, of x²/100 %.

The Two-Object Identity (equal gain/loss %) Two items sold at the same SP, one at +x% profit, one at −x% loss.
Net loss% on total transaction = x²/100
(Always a loss, never a profit, regardless of x.)

Worked: two articles sold at the same SP, profit 10% on one, loss 10% on the other. Net = 100/100 = 1% loss. Sold at 20% each? 400/100 = 4% loss. Sold at 25%? 625/100 = 6.25% loss. Memorise the small cases.

Why does this happen? Because the two articles have different cost prices. The one sold at a loss had a higher cost than its SP; the one sold at a profit had a lower cost than its SP. The two SPs are equal — but the two CPs are not. The higher-CP object dominates and drags the average into loss territory.

3.2The asymmetric variant — different gain and loss percentages

If the percentages are different — sells one at +20%, the other at −10% — the formula breaks down and you must compute directly. Pick a clean SP and back-out two CPs.

CAT 2023 — Gita's two objects (full solution) Hard

Gita sells two objects A and B at the same price such that she makes a profit of 20% on object A and a loss of 10% on object B. If she increases the selling price such that objects A and B are still sold at an equal price and a profit of 10% is made on object B, then the profit made on object A will be nearest to:

  • 42%
  • 47%
  • 45%
  • 49%
STEP 1 · Find the cost prices of A and B Let common SP = 60. (Picked because 1.2 and 0.9 both give clean CPs.)
CP of A = 60/1.20 = 50. CP of B = 60/0.90 = 66.67.
STEP 2 · New scenario — B now at +10% profit New SP of B = 1.10 × 66.67 = 73.33. Since A is also at this new common SP, SP of A = 73.33.
STEP 3 · A's new profit A's profit = (73.33 − 50)/50 = 23.33/50 = 46.66% ≈ 47%.
AnswerOption B · 47%

Teaching note: choosing 60 as the common SP was the key. Picking 100 would give CP of B = 111.11 — workable but messier. Always pick a common SP that makes the two profit/loss multipliers clean dividers.

3.3The dishonest dealer — false weights

A merchant claims to sell at cost price but uses a faulty balance: claims 1 kg, gives 950 g. What's the effective profit percentage?

False Weight Profit If the dealer claims weight W but actually delivers weight w (with w < W) at the cost-price rate per kg:
Profit% = ((W − w)/w) × 100
Note the denominator is w, the actual amount delivered — not the claimed amount.

Worked: claims 1000 g, gives 950 g. Profit% = (1000 − 950)/950 × 100 = 50/950 × 100 ≈ 5.26%. The naive answer "5%" (i.e. 50/1000) is wrong; the dealer's profit is on what he actually parted with.

False weight with markup and discount — CAT-style hybrid Hard

A merchant purchases cloth at Rs. 100 per metre and receives 5 cm of cloth free for every 100 cm purchased. He sells the same cloth at Rs. 110 per metre but cheats his customers by giving 95 cm of cloth for every 100 cm purchased by the customers. If the merchant also offers a 5% discount on the selling price, what is the resulting profit percentage earned by him?

STEP 1 · Effective CP per cm of cloth For every Rs. 100 he spends, he gets 105 cm. So his cost per cm = 100/105 = 20/21 rupees.
STEP 2 · Effective SP per cm He claims to sell 1 metre = 100 cm but only gives 95 cm. He charges Rs. 110 per claimed metre, but with 5% discount that's 110 × 0.95 = Rs. 104.50 for 95 cm. So SP per cm = 104.50/95 = 11/10 = 1.10 rupees.
STEP 3 · Profit% Profit% = ((SP − CP)/CP) × 100 = ((1.10 − 20/21) / (20/21)) × 100 = ((23.1/21 − 20/21) / (20/21)) × 100 = (3.1/20) × 100 = 15.5%.
Answer15.5%

Teaching note: this is a CAT-style hybrid combining three commercial-math tricks at once — false weight on purchase, false weight on sale, and a discount. The discipline of computing "per cm" rates from both directions reveals the structure.

3.4The multi-party transaction

CAT 2023 — Minu, Kanu, Tanu Hard

Minu purchases a pair of sunglasses at Rs. 1000 and sells to Kanu at 20% profit. Then, Kanu sells it back to Minu at 20% loss. Finally, Minu sells the same pair of sunglasses to Tanu. If the total profit made by Minu from all her transactions is Rs. 500, then the percentage of profit made by Minu when she sold the pair of sunglasses to Tanu is:

  • 35.42%
  • 31.25%
  • 52%
  • 26%
STEP 1 · Transaction 1 — Minu sells to Kanu Minu's cost = 1000. SP to Kanu = 1.20 × 1000 = 1200. Minu profit so far = +200.
STEP 2 · Transaction 2 — Kanu sells back to Minu at 20% loss Kanu's CP = 1200. SP = 0.80 × 1200 = 960. Minu now buys back at 960.
STEP 3 · Transaction 3 — Minu sells to Tanu Minu needs total profit of 500, has 200 already from transaction 1. So transaction 3 must contribute 300.
Minu's CP for this sale = 960. SP = 960 + 300 = 1260.
Profit% = 300/960 × 100 = 31.25%.
AnswerOption B · 31.25%

Teaching note: the trap is forgetting that Minu's profit accumulates across transactions. The first profit doesn't reset when she buys back. Track Minu's cumulative rupee position.

Module 04 · The Linear Engine

Simple
interest.

The cleaner, easier of the two interest engines. Linear in time, additive across periods, and CAT's gentle on-ramp to compound interest in the next module.

4.1The core formula

Simple Interest SI = P × R × T / 100
Amount A = P + SI = P × (1 + RT/100)
where R is the rate per period and T is the number of periods.

The defining property of simple interest: the interest is paid only on the original principal. Each period adds the same rupee amount of interest, regardless of how long the money has been deposited. Hence "linear in time".

4.2The split-investment problem — CAT's favourite SI archetype

A person has total capital C; divides it across two or three accounts with different rates; total interest is given; find an unknown rate or principal split. CAT 2022 (Nitu's banks, Mr. Pinto's capital) had two such problems. The technique is always: sum the rupee interests across accounts; equate to the total; solve.

CAT 2022 — Nitu's bank investments Hard

Nitu has an initial capital of Rs. 20,000. Out of this, she invests Rs. 8,000 at 5.5% in bank A, Rs. 5,000 at 5.6% in bank B, and the remaining amount at x% in bank C, each rate being simple interest per annum. Her combined annual interest income from these investments is equal to 5% of the initial capital. If she had invested her entire initial capital in bank C alone, then her annual interest income, in rupees, would have been:

  • 900
  • 700
  • 1000
  • 800
STEP 1 · Set up Total income = 5% × 20,000 = Rs. 1,000.
Income from A = 8,000 × 0.055 = 440.
Income from B = 5,000 × 0.056 = 280.
Income from C = 1,000 − 440 − 280 = 280.
STEP 2 · Find rate x Principal in C = 20,000 − 8,000 − 5,000 = 7,000.
7,000 × x/100 = 280 ⟹ x = 4%.
STEP 3 · Compute hypothetical full-capital income at 4% 20,000 × 4% = Rs. 800.
AnswerOption D · Rs. 800

Teaching note: the "surplus tracking" shortcut. A & B each return slightly more than 5%; their excess pulls them above the 5% blended target. C must therefore return less than 5% to bring the blend down. Strong students can spot the answer in under a minute by tracking the deviations.

4.3SI problems with installments

Less common but appears in mocks: a debt is repaid in equal installments. The total interest is computed as if each installment is a mini-loan held for the relevant period. The key idea: each rupee earns interest only for the time it remained with the borrower.

4.4SI rate-doubling, money-tripling

A standard short problem: "At what rate does a sum double itself in 8 years under simple interest?" Use the multiplier: A/P = 2 = 1 + RT/100 ⟹ RT = 100 ⟹ R = 100/8 = 12.5%.

SI Doubling/Tripling Identities To double: RT = 100
To triple: RT = 200
To become n-fold: RT = (n − 1) × 100

4.5The shortcut for split principals — alligation again

When a sum is split across two SI rates and a blended overall rate is given, alligation (from Volume II) gives the ratio of the splits in under five seconds.

SI with alligation Medium

A man invests a total of Rs. 12,000 — partly at 8% per annum simple interest and partly at 12% per annum simple interest. His total annual interest is Rs. 1,160. How much did he invest at each rate?

STEP 1 · Blended rate Total interest / total capital = 1160/12,000 = 9.67% (i.e. 29/3 %).
STEP 2 · Alligate Endpoints 8 and 12, middle 29/3 ≈ 9.67. Bottom-left = 12 − 29/3 = 7/3. Bottom-right = 29/3 − 8 = 5/3.
Ratio (8% : 12%) = 7 : 5. So 7/12 × 12,000 = 7,000 at 8%, and 5,000 at 12%.
STEP 3 · Verify 7,000 × 0.08 + 5,000 × 0.12 = 560 + 600 = 1160 ✓.
AnswerRs. 7,000 at 8% and Rs. 5,000 at 12%

Module 05 · The Exponential Engine

Compound
interest.

The harder, richer engine. Interest on interest. Each period's interest joins the principal for the next period, producing a multiplier that compounds. CAT loves CI because it tests the same successive-percentage logic students learned in Volume I — disguised as a banking problem.

5.1The core formula

Compound Interest A = P × (1 + R/100)n
CI = A − P
For half-yearly compounding: A = P × (1 + R/200)2n
For quarterly compounding: A = P × (1 + R/400)4n

This is identical to the Module 4 compound-growth formula from Volume I. Same multiplier compounding, same exponential structure. The only difference is that banks talk about "interest" and "rate per annum"; population problems talk about "growth rate per year". The arithmetic is the same.

5.2The two-year shortcut

For exactly two years at rate R%, the compound interest equals:

CI for 2 years — the algebraic shortcut CI₂ = P × (2R/100 + R²/10000)
Equivalently, the multiplier expands to 1 + 2R/100 + R²/10000.

Worked: P = 10,000 at 10% for 2 years. CI = 10,000 × (0.20 + 0.01) = 2,100. Or longhand: 10,000 × 1.21 − 10,000 = 12,100 − 10,000 = 2,100 ✓.

5.3Half-yearly and quarterly compounding

When interest is compounded more frequently than once a year, the effective annual yield exceeds the stated rate. The CAT trap: students forget to double the time period and halve the rate (for half-yearly), or quadruple time and quarter the rate (for quarterly).

The Compounding-Frequency Mantra Compounded n times per year over t years at R% per annum:
A = P × (1 + R/(100n))n × t
Half-yearly: n = 2. Quarterly: n = 4. Monthly: n = 12.

5.4The CI-with-partial-payment problem — CAT 2023 archetype

CAT 2023 — Anil's compounded loan Elite

Anil borrows Rs. 2 lakhs at an interest rate of 8% per annum, compounded half-yearly. He repays Rs. 10,320 at the end of the first year and closes the loan by paying the outstanding amount at the end of the third year. Then, the total interest, in rupees, paid over the three years is nearest to:

  • 45,311
  • 51,311
  • 33,130
  • 40,991
STEP 1 · Identify the half-yearly multiplier R = 8% per annum, compounded half-yearly ⟹ 4% per half-year. Half-year multiplier = 1.04. So annual multiplier = 1.04² = 1.0816.
STEP 2 · Balance after year 1 2,00,000 × 1.0816 = 2,16,320. Anil pays 10,320, leaving 2,16,320 − 10,320 = 2,06,000.
STEP 3 · Compound 2,06,000 for the next 2 years 2,06,000 × 1.0816² = 2,06,000 × 1.16985856 = 2,40,990.86… ≈ 2,40,991.
STEP 4 · Total interest Total paid = 10,320 + 2,40,991 = 2,51,311. Total interest = 2,51,311 − 2,00,000 = 51,311.
AnswerOption B · Rs. 51,311

Teaching note: the killer mechanic is the half-yearly compounding turning an annual 8% into an effective 8.16% yield. Students who miss this end up with option D. Track every multiplier explicitly.

5.5Money-doubling under CI — Rule of 72

A widely useful approximation: a sum compounded at R% per annum doubles in approximately 72/R years. Worked: at 8% per annum, money doubles in 72/8 = 9 years. At 12%, in 6 years. At 6%, in 12 years. Exact for very specific rates, accurate to about ±5% for typical CAT rates.

The Rule of 72 Years to double ≈ 72/R
Useful for mental sanity checks and quick eliminations.

Module 06 · Hybrids

SI vs CI
and hybrid
schemes.

The fine print of how the two engines compare, the famous CI−SI difference identity, and the CAT 2023 hybrid problem that asks a student to compare two parallel investments.

6.1The fundamental asymmetry

For the same P, R, and T (with T > 1), CI > SI, always. Equality holds when T = 1 (one period earns the same interest in both schemes). For T > 1, the difference grows.

Simple interest

Interest only on principal. Linear growth in T. Slower in the long run.

Compound interest

Interest on principal AND on accumulated interest. Exponential growth in T. Pulls away from SI for large T.

6.2The CI−SI difference identity

A formula CAT setters rely on heavily — gives the difference between CI and SI for short time periods cleanly:

CI − SI Difference Identities For 2 years: CI₂ − SI₂ = P × (R/100)²
For 3 years: CI₃ − SI₃ = P × (R/100)² × (3 + R/100)

The 2-year version is the workhorse. Worked: P = 10,000 at 10% for 2 years. SI₂ = 2,000. CI₂ = 2,100. Difference = 100 = P × (R/100)² = 10,000 × 0.01 = 100 ✓.

The 3-year version is uglier but still useful. Worked: P = 10,000 at 10% for 3 years. SI₃ = 3,000. CI₃ = 13,310 − 10,000 = 3,310. Difference = 310 = P × (R/100)² × (3 + R/100) = 10,000 × 0.01 × 3.1 = 310 ✓.

Why the formula? Because the difference is exactly the interest-on-interest component that CI captures but SI doesn't. For 2 years that's just P × R% × R% — one period of interest on one period of interest. For 3 years it's a cleverer combination of three such cross-terms.

6.3The CAT 2023 SI–CI hybrid problem

CAT 2023 — Anil's CI scheme vs Sunil's hybrid Elite

Anil invests Rs. 22,000 for 6 years in a certain scheme with 4% interest per annum, compounded half-yearly. Sunil invests in the same scheme for 5 years, and then reinvests the entire amount received at the end of 5 years for one year at 10% simple interest. If the amounts received by both at the end of 6 years are the same, then the initial investment made by Sunil, in rupees, is:

STEP 1 · Anil's final amount 4% per annum compounded half-yearly = 2% per half-year, over 12 half-years.
Anil's amount = 22,000 × 1.02¹².
STEP 2 · Sunil's final amount Sunil's first 5 years: P × 1.02¹⁰. Then this amount earns 10% SI for 1 year: multiplier = 1.10.
Sunil's amount = P × 1.02¹⁰ × 1.10.
STEP 3 · Equate 22,000 × 1.02¹² = P × 1.02¹⁰ × 1.10
P = 22,000 × 1.02² / 1.10 = 22,000 × 1.0404 / 1.10 = 22,000 × 0.945818… ≈ 20,808.
AnswerRs. 20,808

Teaching note: the elegance is recognising that 1.02¹⁰ cancels from both sides, leaving a much simpler comparison between 1.02² and 1.10. This is structural cancellation — and a hallmark of well-set CAT problems.

6.4The "what rate at what time" comparison problem

A standard CAT mock problem: "What is the difference between CI and SI on Rs. 6,000 for 2 years at 10% per annum?" Use the identity. CI₂ − SI₂ = 6,000 × (0.10)² = 60. Five seconds.

Module 07 · Tactics

Twelve tips,
tricks, and traps.

The accumulated wisdom. What to drill, what to whisper, the traps you will see repeatedly in mocks, and the moves that save thirty seconds per question on the actual paper.

TIP 01 · BASE
Profit is on CP, discount is on MP

The two percentages have different bases. A 20% markup and 20% discount do NOT cancel — they leave you 4% in the loss. Always identify the base before computing.

TIP 02 · MULTIPLIER
SP/CP is one number

Treat SP/CP as a single net multiplier. Then 1 + profit% = multiplier directly. No need to compute markup and discount separately if you only want the bottom line.

TIP 03 · 100
Pick CP = 100 if no rupee figure given

The same trick from Volume I. With CP = 100, every percentage becomes a rupee figure, and arithmetic vanishes. Scale at the end if a final figure is needed.

TIP 04 · TWO OBJECTS
Equal % up and down → always a loss

If two articles sold at the same SP, one at +x% and one at −x%, the net is ALWAYS a loss of x²/100 %. Memorise the small cases — 1%, 4%, 6.25%, 9%, 25%.

TIP 05 · CHAIN
Multi-party problems track cumulative position

In CAT 2023 Minu-Kanu-Tanu type problems, each transaction adds to Minu's running balance. Don't reset on each transaction.

TIP 06 · FREQUENCY
Halve the rate, double the periods

For half-yearly CI: R becomes R/2, T becomes 2T. For quarterly: R/4, 4T. The exponent and the rate scale together. Memorise this and you'll never miss a compounding-frequency question.

TIP 07 · CI−SI
Difference for 2 years = P(R/100)²

Cleanest one-line formula in this volume. Whenever a question gives CI−SI difference for 2 years, plug it in. Five seconds.

TIP 08 · 72
Rule of 72 for sanity checks

Money doubles in approximately 72/R years under CI. Use this for quick eliminations on multiple-choice problems involving doubling, tripling, or quadrupling.

TIP 09 · WORDS
Read transaction direction carefully

"A sells to B at 20% profit" means A's profit; B's CP is whatever A charged. Don't mix up whose profit is whose. Underline the seller in every transaction.

TIP 10 · ALLIGATION
Two-rate SI problems are alligation problems

Whenever capital is split between two SI rates with a known blended return, alligate on the rates. Volume II's cross diagram applies directly.

TIP 11 · WEIGHTS
False weight: divide by what was delivered

Effective profit% in false-weight problems uses what was actually delivered as denominator, not what was claimed. The dealer's gain is on what he parted with.

TIP 12 · VERIFY
Plug back the rupee figure

For complex chains, after solving, compute the full rupee trail one more time. Catches sign and base errors.

7.1Six traps that wreck the median student

Trap 1 — Profit on selling price. A student computes "profit% = (SP − CP)/SP × 100". Wrong. Profit and loss are always on cost price. The wrong formula gives smaller percentages and matches option (B) of distractor sets.

Trap 2 — Markup and discount additive. "Marked up 25%, discount 25%" → student concludes "break-even". Wrong: 1.25 × 0.75 = 0.9375, so 6.25% loss. Multiplier method prevents this.

Trap 3 — Two-object problem solved as "break-even". Equal % gain and loss on equal SPs always loses money. Always.

Trap 4 — SI rate substituted directly for CI. The SI for 3 years at 10% is 30% of P. The CI for 3 years at 10% is 33.1% of P. Different. Always compute each from its own formula.

Trap 5 — Compounded half-yearly: forgetting to adjust the rate. 8% per annum compounded half-yearly is NOT 8% per half-year. It is 4% per half-year. Half the rate, double the periods.

Trap 6 — Treating Discount as profit reduction on CP. A 20% discount does not directly reduce profit by 20% of CP. It reduces SP from MP, which separately affects the SP − CP gap. Compute the chain, not the shortcut.

7.2The two-minute problem checklist

Print this and tape it inside the student's notebook:

  1. Identify the entities. CP, SP, MP for P&L. P, R, T, A for interest.
  2. Underline every transaction direction (who sells to whom).
  3. If no rupee figure is given, set CP = 100 (or P = 100, depending on topic).
  4. Translate every percentage change into a multiplier.
  5. Chain the multipliers: net multiplier = product of stage multipliers.
  6. For SI splits, alligate. For CI partial payments, balance forward.
  7. Verify by computing the full rupee trail one more time.

Q24 · CI from 2-year and 3-year amountsElite

On a certain sum, the compound interest earned at the end of 2 years is Rs. 880, and at the end of 3 years is Rs. 1,456. Find the principal.

STEP 1 · Interest accrued in year 3CI₃ − CI₂ = 1,456 − 880 = 576. This is the interest in year 3 alone.
The year-3 interest is computed on the amount at the start of year 3, which is P + CI₂ = P + 880.
STEP 2 · Find the rateYear 2 alone interest = CI₂ − CI₁. CI₁ = P × R/100. CI₂ = P × ((1+R/100)² − 1). Year 2 interest = CI₂ − CI₁ = P × R/100 × (1 + R/100) − P × R/100 = P × R²/10000.
Also, ratio: year-3 interest / year-2 interest = (P + 880) × R/100 / [P × R/100 × (1+R/100)] = (P + 880)/(P × (1 + R/100)).
But more directly: year-3 = year-2 × (1+R/100). So 576/(year-2 interest) = 1 + R/100.
STEP 3 · Year-2 interestYear-2 interest = year-1 interest × (1+R/100). And year-1 interest = P × R/100.
Take an alternative route. Let r = R/100. CI₂ = P((1+r)² − 1) = P(2r + r²) = 880. CI₃ = P((1+r)³ − 1) = P(3r + 3r² + r³) = 1,456.
Divide: (3r + 3r² + r³)/(2r + r²) = 1,456/880 = 26/15 × something… let's compute: 1456/880 = 1.6545… = 182/110 = 91/55.
Try r = 0.20 (i.e. 20%): (0.6 + 0.12 + 0.008)/(0.4 + 0.04) = 0.728/0.44 = 1.654 ✓.
So r = 0.20, R = 20%. Then P × (0.4 + 0.04) = 880 ⟹ P × 0.44 = 880 ⟹ P = 2,000.
AnswerPrincipal = Rs. 2,000

Everything in this codex distilled. Photocopy; tape into the notebook; review before every mock.

9.1Profit, loss, discount

The Chain MP = CP × (1 + markup%/100)
SP = MP × (1 − discount%/100)
Profit% = (SP − CP)/CP × 100
Loss% = (CP − SP)/CP × 100
Two-Object Identity Two SPs equal, gains x% and loses x% → net loss = x²/100 %
False Weight Profit% = (claimed − actual)/actual × 100
(Denominator is what the dealer actually delivered.)

9.2Simple interest

SI Identities SI = P × R × T / 100
A = P × (1 + RT/100)
Doubling: RT = 100 · Tripling: RT = 200

9.3Compound interest

CI Identities A = P × (1 + R/100)n
Half-yearly: A = P × (1 + R/200)2n
Quarterly: A = P × (1 + R/400)4n
Rule of 72: years to double ≈ 72/R

9.4CI − SI difference identities

The Difference Identities For 2 years: CI − SI = P × (R/100)²
For 3 years: CI − SI = P × (R/100)² × (3 + R/100)

9.5The traps catalogue

  • Profit/loss is on CP, never on SP.
  • Markup is on CP; discount is on MP. Different bases.
  • Equal % gain + equal % loss on two equal SPs → always net loss.
  • Successive discounts multiply; never add.
  • Half-yearly CI: halve R, double n.
  • For SI splits, alligate.
  • In multi-party chains, track cumulative profit, don't reset.

9.6The two-minute checklist

  1. Identify entities (CP, SP, MP for P&L; P, R, T, A for interest).
  2. Underline transaction directions.
  3. Set CP = 100 (or P = 100) if no rupee figure is given.
  4. Translate every percentage change to a multiplier.
  5. Chain the multipliers.
  6. For SI splits, alligate. For CI partials, balance forward.
  7. Verify by walking the rupee trail.
