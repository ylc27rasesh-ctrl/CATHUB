Module 00 · Orientation

Why percentages
quietly decides
your QA score.

A teacher's overview of where this topic sits in the CAT — what to teach, what to skip, and how to frame the entire arithmetic block around this one idea.

Percentages is not a topic. It is a language. Every other arithmetic topic in the CAT — profit and loss, simple and compound interest, mixtures, data interpretation, even some geometry questions about area change — borrows its grammar from percentages. If a student is shaky here, every other chapter wobbles. If a student is fluent here, you have just unlocked roughly forty percent of the Quant section in a single stroke.

The good news for the classroom: this topic is overwhelmingly conceptual, not formula-heavy. There are about six ideas worth knowing and the rest is fluent translation between fractions, decimals, and percentage forms. The bad news: those six ideas hide inside questions about elections, libraries, salaries, tensile strength, mango stocks, and Olympic salary ratios. CAT setters deliberately disguise structure.

0.1How to use this codex in class

The nine modules are sequenced for a roughly 10–12 hour block of teaching: about four sessions if you teach two-and-a-half hours each, or six if you go ninety minutes. Modules 1 and 2 are foundational and should never be skipped, even for strong batches; weak fluency in fraction-percentage equivalents is the single biggest predictor of slowness in this section.

Modules 3 through 5 contain the three classic "engines" CAT runs on — additive change, multiplicative change, and inverse change. Module 6 is where the engines meet real CAT problems. Module 7 collects the tactical shortcuts and the four or five traps you will see the same student fall into year after year. Module 8 is a graded problem bank. Module 9 is what students should photocopy and stick on a wall.

Teaching philosophy. Resist the urge to start with the formula Percentage = (Part/Whole) × 100. Start instead with the idea that a percentage is a portable comparison, like a currency exchange rate. Formulas are then derived, not memorised.

0.2What the CAT actually asks

From a careful scan of the 2016–2025 CAT papers, percentage-based questions fall into a small number of recurring archetypes. Knowing these archetypes by name lets a student pattern-match in under ten seconds:

  • Successive percentage change. Population grows 10%, then falls 20%; price up 25%, then down 25%. The trap is always treating these as additive when they are multiplicative.
  • Chained comparison. "A is 20% more than B, who is 25% less than C, who is 30% more than D…" These resolve in seconds if you assume a clean starting number and walk the chain.
  • Pass-fail / examination problems. Two candidates score different percentages and either fail or pass by some margin. Almost always solvable by two linear equations.
  • Mixture-percentage hybrids. "A 40-litre mixture is 25% milk; how much water to add to make it 20% milk?" Classic CAT favourite. Pure-component conservation is the key.
  • Election / vote-share / share-of-total problems. Multi-step but mechanical: cast votes > valid votes > share splits > back-solve.
  • Profit/discount disguised as percentage. Increasingly common since 2023. The question never says "profit", it says "marked up by 30% then discounted by 10%".
  • Salary / income / expenditure flows. Multiple-period problems involving hikes, savings ratios, and percentage of income.

Classroom diagnostic in two minutes. Ask the batch: "A shirt is marked up by 25% and then sold at a discount of 20%. What is the net change on cost price?" The students who say "5% profit" need to redo Module 1. The students who say "zero" understand multiplicative thinking and can be moved faster. The students who hesitate are your median — and your codex is designed for them.

0.3Tools the student is allowed

The CAT provides an on-screen basic calculator. This calculator is slow and uses click-by-click input. The reflex of reaching for it on every percentage problem is the single biggest time-sink in this section. The codex therefore emphasises mental math and fraction-conversion shortcuts throughout. By the end of Module 2 a student should be able to compute "37.5% of 240" in three seconds — without touching the on-screen pad.

Module 01 · Foundations

What a percentage
actually is.

Building the mental model from zero. Per-cent. Per hundred. A unit of comparison. By the end of this module, students should never again confuse 'percent of' with 'percent more than'.

1.1The literal meaning

The word percent comes from the Latin per centum — "by the hundred". When we say 40 percent, we mean 40 out of every 100. The percent sign % is, historically, just a corruption of the fraction /100.

So at the most literal level:

The Definition A percentage is a fraction with a fixed denominator of 100.
x% = x/100 · therefore 40% = 40/100 = 0.4 = 2/5

That last triple equivalence — percentage ↔ fraction ↔ decimal — is the single most important reflex in this entire topic. Drill it. Drill it until the conversion is unconscious.

Wrong mental model

"Percentage is a number with a percent sign at the end. To find x% of N, I multiply N by x and divide by 100."

Right mental model

"A percentage is a fraction. 40% is just another way of writing 2/5. To find 40% of 75, I find two-fifths of 75 — which is 30."

1.2The three forms — and when to use each

Form 1 — the fraction form

Use whenever the percentage has a clean fraction equivalent (we will memorise these in Module 2). 25% of 80 = (1/4) × 80 = 20. Far faster than 80 × 25 ÷ 100.

Form 2 — the decimal form

Use whenever multiple percentages need to be multiplied together. 20% growth followed by 10% growth = 1.2 × 1.1 = 1.32, i.e. 32% net growth. Decimal form is the language of successive change.

Form 3 — the percent-of-100 form

Use whenever the question gives you ratios and proportions of an unknown total. The trick is to assume the total is 100 — which makes every percentage instantly tangible.

The "assume 100" reflex. Whenever a CAT question says "A class has some girls and some boys; the girls are 40% of the class…" and never gives you a total, mentally write down "class strength = 100" and proceed. You can scale at the end if needed. This single trick saves an enormous amount of algebra.

1.3The two relationships students confuse

This is the single biggest source of error in the early stages of teaching this topic. The same words, in slightly different positions, mean very different things.

"A is 40% of B"

A is a portion of B. A is smaller. The equation is A = 0.4 × B. If B = 200, then A = 80.

"A is 40% more than B"

A exceeds B by 40% of B. A is larger. The equation is A = 1.4 × B. If B = 200, then A = 280.

Train the reflex by reading aloud. Read every percentage phrase out loud, slowly. "Forty percent of" is one thing. "Forty percent more than" is another. "Forty percent less than" is a third. The CAT will weave all three into a single sentence and the student must parse them at reading speed.

1.4Percentage change — the core operation

If something changes from an old value to a new value, the percentage change is computed against the old value. Always. The base of comparison is the starting point, not the ending point, not their average, not their sum.

Percentage change % change = [(New − Old) / Old] × 100
Positive value → increase. Negative value → decrease.

The asymmetry trap. If something rises from 100 to 150, that is a 50% increase. If it then falls from 150 back to 100, that is a 33.33% decrease, not 50%. Same absolute change; different percentage change, because the base changed. CAT loves this asymmetry. Spend ten minutes drilling it in class.

1.5A worked walkthrough to seal the module

Foundation example — chained comparison Easy

In an examination, the score of A was 10% less than that of B. B's score was 25% more than that of C. C's score was 20% less than that of D. If A scored 72, what was D's score?

STEP 1 · Decode the relationships A = 0.9 × B (since A is 10% less than B)
B = 1.25 × C
C = 0.8 × D
STEP 2 · Express A in terms of D A = 0.9 × 1.25 × 0.8 × D = 0.9 × D = 0.9D
(Notice: 1.25 × 0.8 = 1 exactly. That's a hidden teaching moment — 25% up followed by 20% down nets to no change.)
STEP 3 · Solve A = 72, so 0.9D = 72, giving D = 80.
AnswerD scored 80

Teaching note: pause here and ask the class to notice that "25% up then 20% down" cancels exactly. This is a free reciprocal-pair lesson and a perfect lead-in to Module 2.

· In-class drill ·

Have the batch compute these in their heads, no calculator, fifteen seconds each:

30% of 90 · 15% of 200 · 12.5% of 64 · 250 is what % of 1000? · What is 33⅓% of 270?

Answers: 27, 30, 8, 25%, 90.

Module 02 · The Arsenal

The fraction–percentage
reciprocal table.

Twenty equivalents that, once memorised, will let a student solve eighty percent of arithmetic CAT questions without ever touching the on-screen calculator.

If there is one piece of homework you assign for the entire chapter, it should be this: memorise the fractional equivalents of common percentages until they are as automatic as a multiplication table. The student who knows that 1/8 = 12.5% at the speed of recall will outpace the student who computes it every time, by a factor of four.

2.1Why this matters more than any formula

Consider the calculation 37.5% of 240. A student who reaches for the calculator types: 3-7-.-5 × 2-4-0 ÷ 1-0-0. That is ten clicks, probably twelve seconds. A student who knows that 37.5% = 3/8 sees the problem as "three-eighths of 240" and writes 90. One second. Across a 22-question Quant section, this difference adds up to minutes. Minutes are scores.

2.3The structural patterns inside the table

Don't ask the student to memorise twenty-five rows in isolation. Teach the structural patterns and the table memorises itself.

The halving family

Start at 1/2 = 50% and keep halving the denominator: 1/4 = 25%, 1/8 = 12.5%, 1/16 = 6.25%. Each step halves the percentage. Beautiful, clean, and the most useful family because CAT loves these specific values.

The thirds family

1/3 is 33.33%, 2/3 is 66.66%. Halve 1/3 to get 1/6 = 16.66%. Halve again to get 1/12 = 8.33%. Notice the recurring 3s and 6s — that is your tell.

The fifths family

Each fifth is 20%. So 1/5, 2/5, 3/5, 4/5 are 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%. Halve fifths to get tenths: 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%. Halve again to get twentieths: 5% per unit.

Hidden equivalents that win CAT problems

  • 5/6 = 83.33% — appears in problems about "what percentage remains after a 16.66% loss".
  • 1/7 = 14.28% — appears in problems with weekly cycles and is the trickiest "clean" reciprocal because of its repeating pattern.
  • 7/8 = 87.5% — appears in problems about deep discounts and high efficiency rates.
  • 1/9 = 11.11% — paired with the famous n/(n+1) identity (see Module 5).
The classroom test of fluency. When you say "62.5%", the student should not pause and think. They should hear five-eighths in the same way they hear "three" when you say "1 + 2". The pause is the enemy.

2.4How to apply the arsenal

Take a CAT-style question and watch the arsenal turn an algebraic mess into an arithmetic shrug:

Application — the arsenal in action Easy

A man spends 87.5% of his salary. He saves Rs. 750 a month. What is his monthly salary?

RAW APPROACH (slow) Salary × 0.875 = expense, so savings = 0.125 × salary = 750. Salary = 750/0.125 = 6000. Three calculator steps minimum.
ARSENAL APPROACH (fast) The student instantly recognises 87.5% as 7/8. So expenditure is 7/8 of salary; savings is 1/8 of salary. 1/8 of salary = 750, so salary = 750 × 8 = 6000. One mental step.
AnswerRs. 6,000

2.5One percentage relationship that almost never gets taught — but should

The reciprocal identity (n/(n+1) ↔ 1/(n+1)) If a quantity increases by 100/n %, the new value can also be described as: 1 has become (n+1)/n, equivalently, a quantity that decreases from the new value by 100/(n+1) % returns you to the original.

Translated to numbers: if a price rises by 25% (which is 1/4), the same absolute change is a fall of 1/5 = 20% from the new price. This is the entire engine behind a whole genre of CAT problems including the "if expenses rise by x%, by what percentage must consumption fall to keep expenditure unchanged" family. We meet it again in Module 5.

· Arsenal speed-drill ·

Round-robin in class. Teacher calls out the percentage, student calls back the fraction. Anyone who hesitates is out. Last student standing gets to set the next problem.

87.5% · 16⅔% · 37.5% · 8⅓% · 62.5% · 14²⁄₇% · 11.11% · 6.25%

Module 03 · Engines I

Increase, decrease,
and the reversal
asymmetry.

The first engine: single-step percentage change. The thing every CAT setter exploits is that a 25% up and a 25% down are not the same step in opposite directions.

3.1The multiplier method — the only method

Stop teaching percentage change as addition or subtraction. Teach it as multiplication by a factor. This single framing collapses the difficulty of the entire topic by half.

The multiplier framing x% increase → multiply by (1 + x/100)
x% decrease → multiply by (1 − x/100)

Example: 25% increase = ×1.25 · 30% decrease = ×0.70 · 100% increase = ×2 · 50% decrease = ×0.5

The advantage is monumental. Percentage changes in sequence become products of multipliers, and products commute — the order does not matter. We will lean on this in Module 4.

3.2The reversal problem — why up-then-down ≠ original

Take an item worth Rs. 100. Increase by 20%. The new value is Rs. 120. Now decrease by 20%. The decrease is 20% of 120, not of 100, so it is 24, and the final value is Rs. 96 — a net loss of 4%.

This is the most-tested trap in the entire percentage chapter. The student instinct is to cancel "+20" and "−20" and conclude "no change". The teacher's job is to break that instinct using the multiplier framing:

Up-then-down compactly Final = Original × (1 + x/100) × (1 − x/100) = Original × (1 − x²/10000)
Therefore any equal up-then-down (or down-then-up) leaves a net loss of x²/100 %.

Memorise the small cases:

  • ±10% and ∓10% → net loss of 1%
  • ±20% and ∓20% → net loss of 4%
  • ±25% and ∓25% → net loss of 6.25%
  • ±50% and ∓50% → net loss of 25%

3.3Reversal asymmetry — the harder version

What if the up and down percentages are different? CAT loves this. Item goes up by 25%, then down by 20%. Naive student says +5%. Real answer: 1.25 × 0.80 = exactly 1.00 = no change. This is the same reciprocal-pair identity we noted at the end of Module 1.

Reciprocal pairs that cancel exactly

Increase by

100% (×2) — 50% (×1.5) — 33⅓% (×4/3) — 25% (×5/4) — 20% (×6/5) — 14²⁄₇% (×8/7) — 12.5% (×9/8) — 11.11% (×10/9)

Cancelled by decrease of

50% (×½) — 33⅓% (×2/3) — 25% (×3/4) — 20% (×4/5) — 16⅔% (×5/6) — 12.5% (×7/8) — 11.11% (×8/9) — 10% (×9/10)

Notice the pattern: an increase of 1/n is exactly cancelled by a decrease of 1/(n+1). Teach this as a single sentence and the student will recognise it on sight in the exam.

The classic reversal — adapted CAT pattern Medium

The price of petrol increased by 25%. By what percentage must a motorist reduce his consumption so that his monthly fuel expenditure remains unchanged?

STEP 1 · Frame the constraint Expenditure = Price × Consumption. For expenditure to be unchanged, the product of multipliers must equal 1.
(1.25) × (new consumption multiplier) = 1
New consumption multiplier = 1/1.25 = 0.80
STEP 2 · Translate back to percentage A multiplier of 0.80 = a 20% decrease.
SHORTCUT (the arsenal speaks) 25% = 1/4. So the reciprocal pair cancellation: increase of 1/4 ↔ decrease of 1/5 = 20%. Five seconds.
AnswerReduce consumption by 20%

3.4The "by what percent" question

Two phrasings produce the same numbers but require different answers:

"A is what % of B?"

Answer is (A/B) × 100. If A = 60, B = 80: answer is 75%.

"A is what % more/less than B?"

Answer is ((A − B)/B) × 100. If A = 60, B = 80: answer is −25%, i.e. A is 25% less than B.

The trap in CAT problems is mixing these two — describing one relationship in the question stem and asking for the other in the answer. Slow reading wins here.

3.5"By what % does X exceed Y" — a CAT classic

CAT 2019 (adapted) — Amala / Bimala / Kamala Medium

The income of Amala is 20% more than that of Bimala and 20% less than that of Kamala. If Kamala's income goes down by 4% and Bimala's goes up by 10%, then the percentage by which Kamala's income would exceed Bimala's is nearest to:

  • 31
  • 29
  • 28
  • 32
STEP 1 · Assume a clean base for Amala Let Amala's income = 120 (nice number that allows clean fractions both ways).
"Amala is 20% more than Bimala" → Bimala = 120/1.2 = 100.
"Amala is 20% less than Kamala" → Kamala = 120/0.8 = 150.
STEP 2 · Apply the new changes New Kamala = 150 × 0.96 = 144.
New Bimala = 100 × 1.10 = 110.
STEP 3 · Compute the excess (144 − 110)/110 × 100 = 34/110 × 100 ≈ 30.9% ≈ 31%.
AnswerOption A · 31

Teaching note: the deliberate choice of 120 as Amala's base — not 100 — is the lesson. Choosing the right base is half the problem.

Module 04 · Engines II

Successive
percentage
changes.

Two changes in series. Three changes in series. The exact formula, the approximation, and the moment to abandon both in favour of the multiplier.

4.1The fundamental rule

Two successive percentage changes do not add. They multiply, through their multipliers. This is the second-most-common mistake in the topic, and the most common in the chapter on successive change specifically.

Successive change — the exact rule Final = Original × (1 + a/100) × (1 + b/100)

Equivalently, net % change = a + b + (ab)/100
Use positive for increases, negative for decreases.

Worked walkthrough. A population grows 10% in year one and then declines 20% in year two. The net change is:

  • By formula: 10 + (−20) + (10 × −20)/100 = 10 − 20 − 2 = −12%
  • By multiplier: 1.10 × 0.80 = 0.88 → −12%

4.2When to use which

Use the formula a + b + ab/100

When the percentages are clean two-digit numbers and the question asks specifically for the percentage change. Two numbers only. Faster for verbal computation.

Use the multiplier method

When there are three or more successive changes, or when the percentages are messy (12.5%, 16⅔%), or when the question asks for the final value rather than the percentage change. More reliable, scales cleanly.

4.3Three changes and beyond

For three changes a, b, c there is no clean closed-form formula. Just multiply: Net multiplier = (1 + a/100)(1 + b/100)(1 + c/100). This is exactly the kind of problem where the on-screen calculator earns its keep — but only if the multipliers are friendly enough that the multiplication is fast.

Adapted CAT pattern — three-stage change Medium

The tensile strength of a substance A depends on the multiplicative product of four ingredient quantities a, b, c, d. If a is increased by 30%, b decreased by 30%, c decreased by 25%, and d increased by 25%, find the percentage change in A's tensile strength.

STEP 1 · Translate to multipliers a-factor 1.30 · b-factor 0.70 · c-factor 0.75 · d-factor 1.25
STEP 2 · Pair up cleverly Note 1.30 × 0.70 = 0.91. (This is the standard ±30% reversal — a 9% loss.)
Note 1.25 × 0.75 = 0.9375. (Standard ±25% reversal — a 6.25% loss.)
STEP 3 · Combine Net multiplier = 0.91 × 0.9375 = 0.853125
Percentage change = 0.853125 − 1 = −0.146875 ≈ −14.69%
AnswerTensile strength falls by approximately 14.7%

Teaching note: showcase how the two pairing tricks (Module 3) collapse this from four ugly multiplications to two clean ones.

4.4The two-step intuition trap

Many students, after they understand "+10% and −10% leaves you down by 1%", believe they can add the percentages plus a small correction. They cannot, in general.

If the absolute values of the changes are small (each under, say, 5%), then "a + b" approximates the truth well, and the ab/100 correction is small enough to ignore for rough estimation. For larger changes — anything over 10% — the correction term is meaningful and ignoring it will cost you the question. Train the student to recognise when the approximation breaks.

4.5Compound growth — special case of successive change

If a quantity grows by the same percentage r% each period for n periods, the multiplier compounds:

Compound percentage growth Final = Original × (1 + r/100)n

This is the bridge to compound interest, which is just percentage growth applied to money. Module 6 includes a worked example.

Successive growth + decline mix Medium

The value of a property appreciates at 20% per annum for the first two years, then depreciates at 10% per annum for the next two years. If the final value is Rs. 1,16,640, what was the original value?

STEP 1 · Build the multiplier (1.20)² × (0.90)² = 1.44 × 0.81 = 1.1664
STEP 2 · Reverse Original × 1.1664 = 1,16,640
Original = 1,16,640 / 1.1664 = 1,00,000
AnswerRs. 1,00,000

Teaching note: the final value 1,16,640 was chosen specifically to give a round answer. Always look for these "set-up numbers" in CAT — they are the setter's way of confirming you took the intended route.

Module 05 · Engines III

Product constancy
and the replacement
identity.

The third engine — and the one that turns medium CAT questions into one-line shortcuts. If you teach this module well, your students will fear no percentage question.

5.1The principle of product constancy

Many CAT problems boil down to a relationship of the form: A × B = C, where C is held constant. Examples are everywhere:

  • Price × Quantity = Total expenditure (the rupee bill at the petrol pump)
  • Length × Breadth = Area (a field's dimensions changing)
  • Rate × Time = Distance (the speed-distance hybrid)
  • Marked Price × (1 − discount rate) = Selling Price

When one factor changes by x%, the other must change by a corresponding percentage to keep the product constant.

Product constancy identity If A increases by x%, then to keep A × B constant, B must decrease by [x/(100+x)] × 100 %

If A decreases by x%, then to keep A × B constant, B must increase by [x/(100−x)] × 100 %

5.2The reciprocal pair shortcut — using the arsenal

The above identity is rarely worth memorising in its raw form. Use the reciprocal-pair table from Module 3 instead:

Pattern in plain English. A rise of "one over n" is exactly cancelled by a fall of "one over (n+1)". Memorise this single sentence and the entire price-quantity, marked-price-discount, speed-time hybrid family becomes one-liners.

5.3Worked applications

Classic price-consumption problem Easy

The price of sugar rose by 25%. By what percentage should a household reduce its consumption so that the expenditure on sugar remains unchanged?

PATTERN MATCH Price × Quantity = Expenditure. Price up 25% = 1/4 increase. Reciprocal pair: 1/5 decrease.
ANSWER Consumption must fall by 1/5 = 20%.
Answer20%
The reverse direction — adapted CAT pattern Medium

If the price of sugar is reduced by 20%, a customer can buy 5 kg more for Rs. 250. What was the original price of sugar per kg, and what is the new price?

STEP 1 · Reciprocal pair Price falls by 20% (= 1/5) means quantity at the same expenditure rises by 1/4 = 25%.
STEP 2 · Use the absolute change That 25% rise equals 5 kg. So the original quantity bought = 5 / 0.25 = 20 kg.
Original price = 250/20 = Rs. 12.50 per kg.
New price = 12.50 × 0.80 = Rs. 10 per kg.
AnswerOriginal Rs. 12.50; new Rs. 10.00

5.4The replacement family

A second, distinct CAT favourite: a mixture or solution has some pure component diluted by another, and the question asks what percentage to replace or what concentration results.

Successive replacement (concentration) Final concentration = Initial concentration × (1 − r/Total)n
where r = amount removed (and replaced) each time, Total = total volume, n = number of replacements.

A 100-litre milk-water mixture is pure milk. If 20 litres are removed and replaced with water, then this is done a second time: the final milk concentration is 100 × (1 − 20/100)² = 100 × 0.64 = 64%.

Mixture-percentage hybrid Medium

In a mixture of 80 litres, milk and water are in the ratio 7:3. How many litres of water must be added so that the ratio of milk to water becomes 7:5?

STEP 1 · Fix the constants Initial milk = 7/10 × 80 = 56 L. Initial water = 24 L.
When we add water, milk is conserved. The final ratio 7:5 means milk is 7 parts of 12.
STEP 2 · Solve for total 56 = (7/12) × New total → New total = 96 L.
Water added = 96 − 80 = 16 L.
Answer16 litres of water

Teaching note: emphasise that we anchored the calculation on the conserved quantity (milk). Whenever a CAT question has "and then we add/remove X", ask aloud: "What is conserved?"

Module 06 · Applications

Where percentages
actually live
in CAT.

Eight applied themes — elections, exams, profits, populations, salaries, mixtures, taxes, and data interpretation. Each with its specific frame and trapdoor.

6.1Theme — Examination problems

The setup: a student needs a certain percentage to pass; they score some percentage and either fail by some marks or pass with marks to spare. Two unknowns: the maximum marks and the passing marks. Two equations.

Examination — classic two-equation problem Easy

A candidate scoring 30% in an examination fails by 60 marks. Another candidate who scores 50% gets 10 marks more than the minimum pass marks. What are the maximum marks of the examination?

STEP 1 · Set up Let max marks = M, pass marks = P.
From candidate 1: 0.30M = P − 60 ⟹ P = 0.30M + 60
From candidate 2: 0.50M = P + 10 ⟹ P = 0.50M − 10
STEP 2 · Solve 0.30M + 60 = 0.50M − 10 ⟹ 70 = 0.20M ⟹ M = 350
Cross-check: P = 105 + 60 = 165 and also 175 − 10 = 165. ✓
AnswerMaximum marks = 350

6.2Theme — Salary, income, and savings flows

A person earns. They spend a fixed percentage. Income changes; expenditure changes (sometimes by a different percentage); the question asks what happens to savings. The trick is always: Savings = Income − Expenditure. Don't compute the savings percentage of anything; compute the absolute savings before and after.

Salary / savings — classic Easy

A man spends 75% of his income. If his income increases by 20% and his expenditure increases by 10%, by what percentage does his savings change?

STEP 1 · Assume 100 Original income = 100. Expenditure = 75. Savings = 25.
STEP 2 · Apply changes New income = 120. New expenditure = 75 × 1.10 = 82.5. New savings = 120 − 82.5 = 37.5.
STEP 3 · Percentage change in savings (37.5 − 25)/25 × 100 = 50%.
AnswerSavings increase by 50%

6.3Theme — Population, inflation, and depreciation

Same engine as compound growth in Module 4. A town's population is N today; it grows at r% annually; what is it after t years? Answer: N × (1 + r/100)t. Inflation works identically; depreciation uses (1 − r/100) instead.

CAT 2018 (adapted) — fiction-book census Medium

In 2010, a library contained 11,500 books split between fiction and non-fiction. By 2015 the total was 12,760 books. Fiction grew by 10%, non-fiction by 12% in this period. How many fiction books were there in 2015?

STEP 1 · Variables Let F and N be the 2010 counts. F + N = 11,500. After growth: 1.1F + 1.12N = 12,760.
STEP 2 · Solve the system Multiply first equation by 1.1: 1.1F + 1.1N = 12,650.
Subtract from the second: 0.02N = 110 ⟹ N = 5,500.
So F = 6,000. Fiction in 2015 = 6,000 × 1.10 = 6,600.
AnswerFiction books in 2015 = 6,600

6.4Theme — Profit, loss, discount

This is a full topic in its own right, but percentage is the language it speaks. The four entities to keep straight: Cost Price (CP), Selling Price (SP), Marked Price (MP), Discount. The key formulas:

P&L percentage relationships Profit % = (SP − CP)/CP × 100
Loss % = (CP − SP)/CP × 100
SP = MP × (1 − Discount %/100)
MP = CP × (1 + Markup %/100)

Two common confusions. (1) Markup is on cost price; Discount is on marked price. They are not on the same base. (2) "Sold at 20% profit" means SP = 1.2 × CP; "sold at a discount of 20%" means SP = 0.8 × MP. Same percentage, different operations.

6.5Theme — Elections and vote shares

Layered percentages: turnout %, vote share %, valid vote %. The trick is to compute each step on the correct base, top-down: total electorate → votes cast → valid votes → candidate's share.

CAT 2020 (adapted) — election Hard

In an election, four candidates contested. 80% of registered voters cast their votes. One candidate received 30% of the votes cast; the other three split the remaining votes in the ratio 1:2:3. The winner received 2,512 votes more than the runner-up. How many registered voters were there?

STEP 1 · Let total registered voters = V Votes cast = 0.8V. One candidate gets 30% × 0.8V = 0.24V.
Other three share 70% × 0.8V = 0.56V in the ratio 1:2:3 — so they get (1/6, 2/6, 3/6) of 0.56V = (0.0933V, 0.1867V, 0.28V).
STEP 2 · Identify winner and runner-up The four candidates' shares: 0.24V, 0.0933V, 0.1867V, 0.28V.
Winner = 0.28V. Runner-up = 0.24V. Difference = 0.04V.
STEP 3 · Solve 0.04V = 2,512 ⟹ V = 62,800.
AnswerRegistered voters = 62,800

Already met in Module 5. The key habit: identify what's conserved (pure milk, or pure water, or pure alcohol), apply percentage transformations to that quantity only.

The math is trivial; the trap is misreading the chart or confusing the base. Train the student to circle the base year/segment in the question stem before computing anything.

Module 07 · Tactics

Twelve tips,
tricks, and traps.

The accumulated wisdom — what to drill, what to whisper, and the mistakes you will see again and again in classroom mocks.

TIP 01 · BASE
Always assume 100

Whenever a problem doesn't specify a total, assume the total is 100. The percentages become the actual numbers. Scale at the end if the answer asks for an absolute value.

TIP 02 · ARSENAL
Fractions over decimals

Whenever you see 12.5%, 16⅔%, 37.5%, 87.5%, switch immediately to the fraction (1/8, 1/6, 3/8, 7/8). The arithmetic is two to four times faster.

TIP 03 · MULTIPLIER
Translate to multipliers

Don't add or subtract percentages. Turn every "20% increase" into "× 1.20" and the entire chain becomes a single multiplication.

TIP 04 · RECIPROCAL
Up by 1/n cancels down by 1/(n+1)

The single most useful identity. A 25% rise is undone by a 20% fall. A 50% rise is undone by a 33⅓% fall. Memorise the entire family.

TIP 05 · CHANGE
Base of comparison matters

From 100 to 150 is +50%. From 150 back to 100 is −33⅓%, not −50%. The base always changes.

TIP 06 · ALGEBRA
Two unknowns, two equations

The vast majority of "examination", "salary", and "library" problems are linear in two unknowns. Set them up; solve them. Don't try to guess.

TIP 07 · CONSERVATION
Find what's conserved

In mixture and replacement problems, one ingredient is always conserved while others change. Anchor every calculation on the conserved quantity.

TIP 08 · CLEAN NUMBERS
Pick clean base values

If a problem says A is 20% more than B, set B = 100, not 50. If A is 25% more than B, set B = 100 or B = 4. The "right" base makes the arithmetic vanish.

TIP 09 · APPROXIMATION
Estimate before solving

For DI and complex problems, an approximate answer in ten seconds beats an exact answer in two minutes. Use it to eliminate options.

TIP 10 · UNIT
Don't mix % of and % more than

"A is 40% of B" means A = 0.4B. "A is 40% more than B" means A = 1.4B. Read the proposition out loud before translating.

TIP 11 · OPTIONS
Read the options first

If the gap between options is wide, you can rough-estimate. If it's tight, you must compute exactly. The options tell you how careful to be.

TIP 12 · VERIFY
Always sanity-check

Plug your answer back into the problem. If A scored 72 and you derived D = 80, verify that 72 is 10% less than 25% more than 20% less than 80. Five seconds. Catches sign and direction errors.

7.1Five traps that wreck the median student

Trap 1 — Direction confusion. "A is 20% less than B" → A = 0.8B, NOT B = 0.8A. Stronger students still misread this under time pressure. Train the habit of underlining the comparison word ("more"/"less") and circling the reference quantity.

Trap 2 — Base substitution. When a quantity changes through stages, the base of the next percentage is the new value, not the original. Multipliers force this discipline; addition disguises it.

Trap 3 — Percentage of percentage. "30% of 40% of X" is 0.30 × 0.40 × X = 0.12X = 12% of X. Students sometimes add to get 70%. Always multiply.

Trap 4 — Total-vs-partial bases. In election problems, when the question says "30% of the votes cast", verify whether the base is total voters or valid votes or votes cast. Read twice.

Trap 5 — Approximation hubris. The +a +b approximation works only for small a, b. For 30%, 40% changes the cross-term ab/100 is non-trivial. Know when the approximation is allowed and when it isn't.

7.2The two-minute problem checklist

Print this and tape it inside the student's notebook:

  1. Read the question twice. Underline every "more than", "less than", "of".
  2. Identify the base. Assume 100 if no total is given.
  3. Convert messy percentages to fractions.
  4. Translate every change to a multiplier.
  5. Set up equations if there are two unknowns.
  6. Compute. Sanity-check against the options.
  7. Verify by plugging the answer back into one sentence of the original problem.

9.1Core definitions

Percentage x% = x/100 — a fraction with denominator 100.
% of a number x% of N = (x/100) × N
% change % change = (New − Old)/Old × 100
% by which A exceeds B ((A − B)/B) × 100

9.2The multiplier shortcuts

  • x% increase → multiply by (1 + x/100)
  • x% decrease → multiply by (1 − x/100)
  • Two successive changes → net = a + b + ab/100
  • Equal up–down → net loss = x²/100 %
  • Compound, same rate r% for n periods → (1 + r/100)n
  • Replacement n times → final concentration = initial × (1 − r/Total)n

9.5The two-minute problem checklist

  1. Read twice. Underline "more than", "less than", "of".
  2. Set base = 100 if no total is given.
  3. Convert messy percentages to fractions.
  4. Translate every change to a multiplier.
  5. If two unknowns, set up two equations.
  6. Compute. Match against options.
  7. Plug back the answer to verify.

9.6What to drill — recommended sequence

  1. Day 1: Arsenal table — fraction-percentage conversions, 30 minutes.
  2. Day 2: Reciprocal pairs and reversal asymmetry, 45 minutes.
  3. Day 3: Worked easy and medium problems from the bank, 90 minutes.
  4. Day 4: Hard tier and elite tier — at least three each.
  5. Day 5: Sectional mock (10 percentage-themed questions in 18 minutes).
  6. Weekly: revisit the elite tier and the traps. Speed builds through repetition.
One last thing. Tell your students that percentage is the only CAT chapter where the difference between a sixty-percentile student and a ninety-percentile student is almost entirely about speed, not concept. Both know what to do. The faster one converts to fractions, multiplies multipliers, and sees the answer. The slower one is still typing into the on-screen calculator. Drill the arsenal until it is instinct.
