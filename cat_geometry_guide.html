Module 00 · Orientation

The visual
half of Quant.

Geometry is where every CAT student's pen-and-paper habit shows. Draw the figure, mark the data, identify the theorem. The discipline that decides marks.

Geometry on the CAT is the chapter where pen-and-paper discipline matters most. A typical CAT paper has three or four geometry questions across triangles, circles, polygons, and coordinate geometry, plus one or two mensuration questions involving areas and volumes. Together that's roughly five of the twenty-two Quant questions — and these are also the questions with the highest no-attempt rate in the candidate pool, because geometry rewards visualisation in a way no other Quant topic does.

This codex is built around three core habits. First, always draw the figure — even when the question looks "simple enough to do in your head", the figure prevents misreadings. Second, mark all data on the figure — angles, lengths, parallels, perpendiculars. Third, name the theorem before the calculation — Pythagoras, similar triangles, inscribed angle, tangent-secant. With these three habits, every CAT geometry question becomes a one-or-two-step application.

0.1What this codex emphasises

Geometry on the CAT is not about proving theorems — it is about applying them. The student is expected to know that the angle subtended by a chord at the centre is twice the angle subtended at the circumference, and to recognise when a figure invites that theorem. Recognition is the skill; the rest is arithmetic.

Teaching philosophy. Draw first, label second, name the theorem third, compute last. Skip any step and the answer becomes a guess.

Module 01 · Triangles

The building block
of geometry.

Every polygon is made of triangles. Pythagoras, similarity, the four standard centres, and the special triangles — these are the workhorses of CAT geometry.

Triangles deserve their own module because everything else in geometry decomposes to triangles. The Pythagorean theorem, the similarity criteria, the angle-bisector theorem, the formulas for area in terms of sides — all live here. This module covers the essentials in the order that a CAT problem would test them.

1.1Classifying triangles

By sides: equilateral (all three equal), isosceles (two equal), scalene (none equal). By angles: acute (all angles < 90°), right (one angle = 90°), obtuse (one angle > 90°).

Triangle inequality The sum of any two sides exceeds the third: |b − c| < a < b + c

This inequality is a CAT favourite for problems like "the sides of a triangle are 5, 7, and x. Find the integer range of x." Answer: |7 − 5| < x < 7 + 5, so 2 < x < 12. Integer values: 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 — nine values.

1.2Pythagoras and Pythagorean triples

Pythagorean theorem In a right triangle with legs a, b and hypotenuse c: a² + b² = c²

Memorise the common Pythagorean triples: (3,4,5), (5,12,13), (8,15,17), (7,24,25), (9,40,41), (20,21,29). Any multiple of these is also a triple — (6,8,10), (9,12,15) etc. Recognising a triple saves the arithmetic of computing a square root.

The triple habit. Whenever a CAT problem gives two sides of a right triangle as recognisable triple members, the third is immediate. Save the algebra.

1.3Similarity

Two triangles are similar (∼) if their corresponding angles are equal. Equivalently, their corresponding sides are in the same ratio. The three similarity criteria:

  • AA (Angle-Angle): Two pairs of equal angles ⇒ similar.
  • SAS (Side-Angle-Side): Two pairs of sides in equal ratio with included angle equal ⇒ similar.
  • SSS (Side-Side-Side): All three pairs of sides in equal ratio ⇒ similar.
Similarity consequences If △ABC ∼ △DEF with ratio k = AB/DE:
Ratio of sides = k
Ratio of perimeters = k
Ratio of areas = k²
Ratio of altitudes / medians / inradius / circumradius = k

1.4Area formulas

Area of a triangle — multiple formulas A = (1/2) × base × height
A = (1/2) × ab × sin C (two sides & included angle)
A = √(s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)) (Heron's, s = semi-perimeter)
A = rs (r = inradius, s = semi-perimeter)
A = abc / (4R) (R = circumradius)

1.5The four centres

CentreDefinitionNotable
Centroid (G)Intersection of mediansDivides each median 2:1 from vertex
Orthocentre (H)Intersection of altitudesInside acute, on right, outside obtuse
Circumcentre (O)Intersection of perpendicular bisectorsEquidistant from all 3 vertices

The three centres O, G, H lie on a single line called the Euler line, with G dividing OH in ratio 1:2.

1.6Special triangles

Equilateral triangle of side a Area = (√3/4) × a² · Height = (√3/2) × a
Inradius = a/(2√3) · Circumradius = a/√3
30-60-90 and 45-45-90 triangles 30-60-90: sides in ratio 1 : √3 : 2
45-45-90: sides in ratio 1 : 1 : √2

1.7Angle bisector theorem

Angle bisector theorem An angle bisector from vertex A divides the opposite side BC at point D such that:
BD / DC = AB / AC

So the angle bisector divides the opposite side in the ratio of the adjacent sides — an elegant and frequently-used CAT fact.

Module 02 · Circles

The theorems
around a centre.

Inscribed angle, tangent-secant, power of a point. Three or four theorems cover almost every CAT circle question.

A circle is the locus of points equidistant from a centre. From this simple definition, an unreasonable amount of structure emerges — inscribed angles, tangent lengths, cyclic quadrilaterals, the power of a point. CAT circle questions almost always reduce to one of three or four named theorems. This module covers them in the order they appear most often.

2.1Basic terms

Radius (r): distance from centre to any point on circle. Diameter (d) = 2r. Chord: any line segment with both endpoints on the circle. Arc: a portion of the circle between two points. Sector: a "pie slice" bounded by two radii and an arc. Segment: a region bounded by a chord and an arc.

2.2Length and area formulas

Circle — circumference and area Circumference = 2πr · Area = πr²
Sector with central angle θ (degrees) Arc length = (θ/360) × 2πr
Sector area = (θ/360) × πr²

2.3The inscribed angle theorem

Inscribed angle theorem The angle subtended by a chord at the centre is twice the angle subtended at any point on the major arc.
Consequence: All angles inscribed in the same arc are equal.
Special case: An angle inscribed in a semicircle is 90°.

The last consequence — "angle in a semicircle is a right angle" — is one of the most-used facts in CAT geometry. Whenever a problem mentions a diameter and a point on the circle, expect a right angle.

2.4Tangent properties

  • A tangent at any point is perpendicular to the radius at that point.
  • From an external point, the two tangent lengths are equal.
  • Tangent-chord angle = inscribed angle in alternate segment.

2.5Power of a point

Power of a point For any point P and a circle, draw any line through P meeting the circle at A and B:
PA × PB is constant (the "power" of P).
If P is outside and PT is tangent length: PT² = PA × PB.
If P is inside with two chords AB, CD through P: PA × PB = PC × PD.

2.6Cyclic quadrilateral

A quadrilateral whose four vertices lie on a single circle. The key property:

Cyclic quadrilateral Opposite angles are supplementary: A + C = 180°, B + D = 180°.
Ptolemy: AC × BD = AB × CD + AD × BC.

Module 03 · Polygons & Quadrilaterals

From three sides
to many.

Quadrilaterals — square, rectangle, parallelogram, rhombus, trapezium — each with its own area formula. Then the general polygon: angle sum, diagonal count, regular polygon area.

Quadrilaterals are the second most-tested polygon family after triangles. Each has a specific definition, a specific area formula, and a specific CAT question type. This module covers the five standard quadrilaterals, then the general polygon results — angle sum, diagonal count, and regular-polygon area.

3.1The five standard quadrilaterals

TypeDefining propertyArea
SquareAll sides equal, all angles 90°a²
RectangleOpposite sides equal, all angles 90°length × breadth
ParallelogramOpposite sides parallel and equalbase × height
RhombusAll sides equal, opposite sides parallel(1/2) × d₁ × d₂
TrapeziumOne pair of parallel sides(1/2) × (a + b) × h

3.2Diagonals and special properties

  • Square: diagonals equal, perpendicular, bisect each other. Length = a√2.
  • Rectangle: diagonals equal, bisect each other (not perpendicular in general).
  • Parallelogram: diagonals bisect each other (neither equal nor perpendicular in general).
  • Rhombus: diagonals perpendicular bisectors of each other (not equal in general).

3.3The general polygon

Polygon angle sums For an n-sided polygon:
Sum of interior angles = (n − 2) × 180°
Sum of exterior angles = 360° (always, regardless of n)
Number of diagonals = n(n − 3)/2

For a regular polygon (all sides and angles equal):

Regular polygon Each interior angle = (n − 2) × 180° / n
Each exterior angle = 360° / n

3.4The regular hexagon

A regular hexagon of side a has area (3√3 / 2) × a². It is exactly six equilateral triangles glued together — so its area is 6 × (√3/4) × a² = (3√3/2) × a².

Module 04 · Coordinate Geometry

Geometry by
numbers.

When the figure has coordinates, algebra takes over. Distance, slope, intersection — the algebraic engine that solves geometry without drawing.

Coordinate geometry translates geometric configurations into algebraic ones. Points become ordered pairs, lines become equations, areas become determinants. The CAT uses coordinate geometry sparingly — usually one question per paper — but when it does, the techniques are highly standard and reward fluency with three or four formulas.

4.1Distance and section

Distance and division of line segment Distance between (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂):
d = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]
Midpoint: M = ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2)
Section in ratio m:n (internal): ((mx₂ + nx₁)/(m+n), (my₂ + ny₁)/(m+n))

4.2The line

A line in the plane can be written in several equivalent forms:

FormEquationUse
Slope-intercepty = mx + cm = slope, c = y-intercept
Point-slopey − y₁ = m(x − x₁)Through (x₁, y₁) with slope m
Two-point(y − y₁)/(y₂ − y₁) = (x − x₁)/(x₂ − x₁)Through two points
Interceptx/a + y/b = 1x-intercept a, y-intercept b
GeneralAx + By + C = 0Slope = −A/B

4.3Slope, parallel, perpendicular

Slope relations Slope m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁)
Two lines parallel ⇔ m₁ = m₂
Two lines perpendicular ⇔ m₁ × m₂ = −1 (when neither vertical)

4.4Distance from a point to a line

Point-to-line distance For line Ax + By + C = 0 and point (x₀, y₀):
d = |Ax₀ + By₀ + C| / √(A² + B²)

4.5Area by coordinates

Area of triangle by coordinates For vertices (x₁, y₁), (x₂, y₂), (x₃, y₃):
A = (1/2)|x₁(y₂ − y₃) + x₂(y₃ − y₁) + x₃(y₁ − y₂)|

4.6The circle equation

Standard circle Circle of radius r centred at (h, k):
(x − h)² + (y − k)² = r²

Module 05 · Mensuration 2D

Areas of
everything.

Areas of standard shapes, plus the composite-figure technique — break the irregular into the regular, add the pieces.

Mensuration covers measurement of areas (2D) and volumes (3D). The 2D formulas are nearly all of school memorisation, but CAT setters compose them into shapes that need careful decomposition. This module gives the standard formulas in one consolidated reference, then teaches the decomposition habit.

5.2Composite figures · the decomposition habit

CAT mensuration questions often involve irregular shapes — a square with a circular cutout, two overlapping triangles, a path around a garden. The technique is always the same:

  1. Decompose the irregular figure into standard pieces.
  2. Compute each piece's area using the standard formula.
  3. Add (for assembled shapes) or subtract (for cutouts).

Worked example. "A rectangular field 20m × 30m has a circular pond of radius 5m at its centre. Find the area outside the pond." Rectangle area = 600. Pond area = π × 25 = 25π ≈ 78.5. Outside = 600 − 25π ≈ 521.5 m².

5.3The path-around-a-figure pattern

Classic CAT: "A rectangular garden is 30 m by 20 m. A 2-m-wide path is built around it (outside). Find the area of the path."

Outer rectangle: (30 + 4) × (20 + 4) = 34 × 24 = 816. Inner = 600. Path area = 816 − 600 = 216 m².

Module 06 · Mensuration 3D

The solids
that store volume.

Cubes, cuboids, cylinders, cones, spheres, prisms, pyramids. Each has a volume formula and a surface-area formula. Master both.

Three-dimensional mensuration on the CAT covers seven standard solids. For each, you need three quantities: the volume, the lateral surface area (LSA) — the side surface excluding top and bottom — and the total surface area (TSA). This module gives all twenty-one formulas in one consolidated table.

6.1The standard solids — volume and surface area

SolidVolumeTSALSA
Cube (side a)a³6a²4a²
Cuboid (l, b, h)lbh2(lb + bh + lh)2h(l + b)
Cylinder (r, h)πr²h2πr(r + h)2πrh
Cone (r, h, slant ℓ)(1/3)πr²hπr(r + ℓ)πrℓ
Sphere (r)(4/3)πr³4πr²4πr²
Hemisphere (r)(2/3)πr³3πr²2πr²

For the cone, the slant height ℓ satisfies ℓ² = r² + h² by Pythagoras.

6.2The cuboid diagonal

Space diagonal of a cuboid d = √(l² + b² + h²)
For a cube of side a: d = a√3

6.3Combined and modified solids

CAT loves combined-solid problems. A typical example: "A hemisphere of radius 7 cm sits on top of a cylinder of the same radius and height 10 cm. Find the total volume."

Volume = cylinder + hemisphere = π × 49 × 10 + (2/3) × π × 343 = 490π + (686/3)π = (1470 + 686)/3 × π = 2156/3 × π ≈ 2257.7 cm³.

6.4The melting / recasting pattern

A frequent CAT setup: "A solid metal cylinder of radius 6 cm and height 12 cm is melted and recast into spherical balls of radius 1 cm. How many balls?"

Volume is conserved. Cylinder volume = π × 36 × 12 = 432π. Ball volume = (4/3)π × 1 = 4π/3. Number = 432π / (4π/3) = 432 × 3/4 = 324 balls.

Volume conservation. Whenever a CAT problem involves melting, recasting, or transferring liquid between containers, equate the volumes. Surface area is not conserved in any of these processes; only volume is.

Module 07 · Tips & Traps

The figure
errors.

Geometry traps are mostly diagram traps. Eight of them, each named.

TRAP 01
Drawing not to scale

The figure provided in CAT papers is "not to scale". Don't measure with a ruler — use only the data given. Trust the numerical labels, never the visual proportions.

TRAP 02
Forgetting the triangle inequality

When a problem asks for "possible side lengths", check the triangle inequality. A triangle with sides 2, 3, 6 is impossible — 2 + 3 < 6.

TRAP 03
Ratio of areas vs ratio of sides

If similar triangles have side ratio k, their area ratio is k², not k. Off-by-square is the commonest similarity error.

TRAP 04
Confusing radius and diameter

Circumference and area use radius. Many CAT setups give diameter; always halve before substituting.

TRAP 05
Tangent length is not radius

The tangent at a point and the radius to that point are perpendicular. The tangent length from an external point is computed by Pythagoras, not by setting it equal to the radius.

TRAP 06
Sum of polygon angles

The sum of interior angles is (n − 2) × 180°. The sum of exterior angles is always 360°, regardless of n.

TRAP 07
Cone slant vs height

The cone's volume uses height h. The lateral surface area uses slant ℓ. ℓ² = r² + h². Many candidates plug ℓ where h belongs.

TRAP 08
Volume vs surface in melting

When melting/recasting, volume is conserved but surface area is not. Don't equate surface areas before and after.
